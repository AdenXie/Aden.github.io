Introduction: This is our fourth article on AI capital expenditure. The first article‘AI: The Mainstay of Capital Expenditure’A discussion on how computing infrastructure drives macroeconomic growth in the US; Part Two‘In the same week, macro and micro perspectives delivered conflicting verdicts on AI capital expenditure’This piece documents the dichotomy between the view that GDP is ‘very strong’ and the view that, during the earnings season, shares are ‘too expensive’; Part Three‘The Political Calculus of AI Capital Expenditure in the Wake of the Mid-Term Elections’The impact of political factors is felt at state level rather than at federal level. On 5 and 6 November 2026, these three factors were simultaneously priced in by the market within a 24-hour period:The stock market is being valued on the basis of liquidation, the bond market on the basis of solvency, and the non-farm payrolls data has dashed any hopes of an interest rate cut.This article seeks to answer the question of whether this sharp fall is the result of a bubble bursting, or whether something else has simply been priced for the first time.

I. 5 November: It is not that the bubble has burst, but that political risk has been quantified for the first time

First, explain clearly what happened that day, and then explain why.

US stock market close on Thursday, 5 November 2026:

Index decline Closing Prices and Notes
Nasdaq Composite Index -5.52% Closed at 24,315.00, entering a technical correction zone
S&P 500 Index -4.36% Worst single day since the tariff shock on ‘Liberation Day’ in April 2025
Dow Jones Industrial Average -3.44% It closed at 51,523.06, down by more than 1,800 points

There are two distinct methods of calculating market capitalisation; both must be considered in parallel rather than choosing one over the other:The *Wall Street Journal* and CNN cite a figure of $2.5 trillion, described as a single-day record; Reuters cites a figure of $3 trillion, but this only includes S&P 500 constituents. The composition of the two differs, so they cannot be added together nor can they be used as substitutes for one another. Yahoo Finance provides a historical context: this is The worst trading day following a mid-term election since 1896。

Safe-haven assets moved in the opposite direction to equities: on the day, the bond market and gold both rose, the yield on the 10-year US Treasury fell to its lowest level since September, and the VIX closed at 24.23.

It wasn’t demand that triggered the surge, but a percentage figure

This is the most important conclusion of this article, and it is also the point that has been widely misreported by the media this week.

The vast majority of reports described 11-05 as ‘the AI bubble has finally burst’. But the figure that really set things alight was an estimate that emerged after the election results were announced—For the first time, a percentage has been put on the data centre capacity that can be deferred for political reasons.

  • Goldman Sachs: approximately 45 per cent. Published on 4 November, the day before the sharp fall. It is estimated that Democratic victories at state and local levels could lead to temporary project suspensions, affecting approximately 45 per cent of the planned expansion in US data centre capacity.
  • Bloomberg: approximately 40 per cent. Published on 6 November, the day after the sharp fall. The scope is narrower, counting only the capacity in schemes ‘located in states where the new governor has pledged to halt or suspend them’.

It is essential to get the chronology right here, otherwise the cause and effect will be reversed: Goldman Sachs’ 45 per cent figure, which came before the crash, was the trigger; Bloomberg’s 40 per cent figure, which came after the crash, was the review. It is not strictly accurate to describe the two figures as having ‘triggered the crash together’—on 5 November, the figure circulating in the market was the one from Goldman Sachs.

However, the two figures are worth mentioning side by side, as they point to the same conclusion:It is not that demand has disappeared; rather, this is the first time that the notion that ‘this portion of demand may have been postponed due to political processes’ has been quantified. The difference between 45 per cent and 40 per cent is not down to a calculation error, but rather to a difference in the denominators: Goldman Sachs included the risk of shutdowns in the next tier down, whilst Bloomberg only counted those for which the governor had made a public commitment. The day after the sharp fall, Bloomberg carried out an independent calculation using a narrower scope, arriving at 40 per cent—This means that even using the most conservative calculation, 40 per cent of the planned capacity falls within the political risk zone. Which version is closer to reality will depend on how the State Parliament formulates the provisions.

Figure 15 November: The chain of events leading to the crash. The trigger was the first quantification of political risk—Goldman Sachs’ estimate of approximately 45 per cent, published the day before the crash (4 November), served as the trigger, whilst Bloomberg’s estimate of approximately 40 per cent, published the following day (6 November), constituted an independent review; The transmission stage involved a simultaneous drain of resources across the computing power and electricity supply chains; at the close, all three indices fell in the same direction, whilst the scale of the market capitalisation loss is reported under two distinct statistical methodologies—$2.5 trillion and $3 trillion—which are not interchangeable.

The chain of events has laid bare the market’s genuine concerns

The ranking of the declines is, in itself, a diagnostic report:

The closer the sector is to computing chips, the steeper the decline; the power and equipment sectors saw the next steepest declines. This trend suggests that the market is squeezing valuations, not orders—if the market truly believed that demand was set to disappear, the power sector should have fallen even more sharply than the chip sector, as power stations follow the lead of data centres. Yet this has not been the case.

As an aside, here is a piece of secondary data: figures cited by Yahoo Finance show that retail investors made net sales of approximately US$358 million ($358M) on that day, the largest single-day figure since 2020. We have been unable to locate a primary source for this figure; it is provided solely as an indication of market sentiment and is not intended to support any specific argument.

Both interpretations must be presented.

On the same day, there were two completely opposing interpretations circulating in the market; this article does not take sides on behalf of the reader:

First interpretation: This is a speed bump, not a roadblock. Yardeni Research has maintained its year-end target of 7,900 points for the S&P 500 and described Thursday’s sharp fall as a ‘buying opportunity’. Its reasoning is that no data points on the demand side for AI have deteriorated; the decline is merely a valuation reset caused by political noise.

A second interpretation: the language of the credit market is ‘no mas’ (that’s it). This school of thought focuses not on price-to-earnings ratios, but on Neocloud’s funding costs, CDS spreads and the availability of project finance. In their view, 11-05 simply marks the point at which the stock market finally acknowledged what the bond market had been saying for three months.

This journal’s view is that these two interpretations describe two sides of the same issue, but the market has conflated them into a single matter. Please refer to Section 3 for further details.

II. 6 November: The hourly employment figures put an end to hopes of an interest rate cut

On the morning of Friday, 6 November, the US Bureau of Labour Statistics released the October employment figures. The impact of this one-hour period was of greater structural significance than the sharp fall seen the previous day.

Market pricing for the FOMC meeting on 8–9 December immediately shifted:The probability of a 25-basis-point interest rate rise rose from around 67 per cent on Thursday to around 80 per cent on Friday(Reuters, citing market pricing). Reuters’ exact wording was: “A strengthening labour market has kept the Federal Reserve on track for a rate rise in December.”

Another Reuters report published on the same day had an even more straightforward headline – ‘Dollar surges after strong non-farm payrolls figures’ – with the subheading stating that ‘the probability of a rate rise in December has risen to nearly 80 per cent’.

Kathy Bostjancic, Chief Economist at Nationwide, has also publicly expressed her support for a rate rise in December.

Here is a detail that is easily overlooked: The figure of 165,000 in October not only exceeded expectations, but alsowell above the average of 48,000 new cases per month over the past 12 months(According to the BLS’s original terminology). In other words, this is not merely ‘slightly better than expected’, but a figure that has suddenly emerged from a labour market that was verging on stagnation. The combined figures for August and September were revised upwards by 84,000, further reinforcing the view that ‘the summer slump was temporary’.

Left: Market pricing for a 25-basis-point rate hike at the December FOMC meeting rose from approximately 67 per cent on 5 November to approximately 80 per cent on 6 November; Right: The CoreWeave 5-year CDS spread has crossed the 1,000 basis point threshold at which S&P Global defines a ‘credit distress’.

On this occasion, our previous assessment has been confirmed, but the growth is coming from elsewhere.

We are in‘In the same week, macro and micro perspectives delivered conflicting verdicts on AI capital expenditure’It says: ‘Quarterly inflation has fallen to 2.1 per cent, but stood at 3.6 per cent in September alone; GDP growth was 3.4 per cent, and investment in AI remains robust—’There is no reason to cut interest rates, but there are two reasons to raise them further. The non-farm payrolls data on 6 November turned this assessment from ‘analysis’ into ‘pricing’.

ButThe real significance lies not in the probability of a rate rise itself, but in whose costs will be driven up as a result of such a rise. If interest rates are indeed raised in December, it will not be the giants that rely on operating cash flow to stay afloat that will be hardest hit, but rather the ‘neocloud’ companies that must continually tap the bond market for refinancing—their funding costs have already risen ahead of the policy rate. This will be covered in the next section.

III. The credit market cast a vote of no confidence earlier and more decisively than the stock market

If 5 November marked a reckoning for the stock market, then the credit market had already undergone its own reckoning back in October; it’s just that nobody took any notice.

Gavan Nolan of S&P Global has provided a practical benchmark: when CDS spreads exceed 1,000 basis points, the issuer enters the ‘distressed’ category. CoreWeave has crossed the line.

This is not an isolated case. Nebius holds $8 billion ($8B) in cash whilst carrying $8.5 billion ($8.5B) in debt; Applied Digital has debts of approximately $6.4 billion ($6.4B). CoreWeave is due to publish its Q3 results on 10 November; its previously provided guidance for interest expense was between US$860 million and US$940 million, with capital expenditure guidance of US$35 billion to US$39 billion ($35B–$39B).

Structural risks identified by Deutsche Bank

Reid, a credit research analyst at Deutsche Bank, highlighted a point that is even more cause for concern than the CDS figures: CoreWeave’s order backlog exceeds US$100 billion ($100B), whilstAround two-thirds of the counterparties are investment-grade companies— Microsoft, Meta, IBM, Caterpillar.

Whilst this structure may sound like a good thing, it actually conceals a transmission mechanism:If CoreWeave’s rating were downgraded to below BBB, funds that are restricted to holding investment-grade bonds (such as insurance companies, pension funds and some money market funds) would be forced to sell. It is not because they believe CoreWeave will default, but because their investment mandates do not permit the holding of non-investment-grade bonds. This is a mechanical sell-off, unrelated to any judgement.

Funding is also becoming more expensive for the tech giants, though it hasn’t yet reached a critical point.

During the same week, the funding moves by the industry giants were also noteworthy:

  • Meta: The market had originally expected a bond issue of between US$20 billion and US$25 billion ($20B–$25B), but the actual five-tranche issue totalled US$15 billion ($15B) (Reuters figures; the target range reported by CNBC on 3 November was US$20 billion to US$25 billion; both figures are presented side by side). The 2026 capital expenditure guidance remains at US$135 billion to US$145 billion ($135B–$145B).
  • Amazon: A supplier financing facility of up to US$80 billion ($80B) was disclosed in the 10-Q filing, with funds available for drawdown from the first quarter of 2027; a credit facility of US$17.5 billion ($17.5B) was secured in June.

This journal’s assessment (marked as ‘inference’)

Existing cash flows remain unaffected, whilst the cost of additional funding has risen; yet the market is pricing the latter as if it were the former.

This statement needs to be broken down and explained clearly:

  1. Existing stock: Long-term contracts already signed, projects already under construction, and electricity prices already locked in—these cash flows are unaffected by the interest rate rises on 5 November or in December. In this respect, Yardeni is correct when he says that ‘demand has not changed’.
  2. Incremental portion: The cost of financing new projects, the pricing of newly signed contracts, and the cost of maturing debt requiring rollover refinancing—these costs are already rising, and interest rate rises will push them even higher.
  3. What the market is doing: Factor in the bad news from point 2 in a single instalment, using the valuation method set out in point 1.This is a revaluation, not a collapse in fundamentals. Conversely, the opposite is also true: if the bad news mentioned in point 2 proves not to have trickled down to point 1 (for example, if the giants absorb all the new demand using their own cash flow), then this downturn is an overshoot—which is precisely what Yardeni is betting on.

Which of the two is correct? The results from 11–10 and 11–17 will provide the first set of answers.

IV. Elections are the trigger, not the bullet

It is essential to describe the chain of cause and effect accurately:The results of the mid-term elections provided the reason to pull the trigger, but they were not the bullet itself.

These four factors are all at play; had any one of them been missing, the fall would not have been so steep:

  1. Political constraints have been quantified for the first time(Goldman Sachs: approximately 45 per cent, published on 4 November). Prior to this, the statement that ‘data centres might be halted for political reasons’ was a qualitative assessment that could be diluted by the discount rate; with the introduction of a percentage figure, it became a variable that could be modelled. Bloomberg’s review the following day, using a narrower scope, arrived at a figure of 40 per cent, indicating that this was not a conclusion derived solely from Goldman Sachs’ calculations.
  2. Positions are extremely crowded following the earnings season. Throughout the third quarter, capital flowed unidirectionally into the AI computing power sector, meaning that the marginal impact of any negative news would be magnified.
  3. The credit market has priced this in ahead of time. CDS spreads and bond yields had already turned as early as October; the stock market merely made up for this gap on 5 November.
  4. The buffer provided by low-cost funding has been eliminated. The probability of a rate rise in December has jumped from 67 per cent to 80 per cent, meaning that the option of ‘just holding out for a while, as funding is cheap anyway’ is no longer on the table.

It is only when these four are placed side by side that they form 11-05. Attributing this fall solely to the election would lead to erroneous conclusions—such as the belief that the market will automatically recover once the political furore has subsided.

Texas provides the most direct evidence:Republican Governor Abbott had already ordered a suspension of data centre licences back in September(Source: Announcement by the Office of the Governor of Texas, 21 September 2026), which was two months before the Democrats’ election victory. Meanwhile, the newly elected Governor of Texas, Hinojosa, stated in an interview with CNN’s Jake Tapper on the afternoon of 5 November that projects which had already been approved would be allowed to be completed – according to him, these projects account for ‘almost all’ of Texas’s planned capacity for 2027; Hinojosa also stated that he would push for Abbott’s suspension order to be extended until the state legislature’s session next year (Texas Tribune, 4 November 2026).

In other words: opposition to data centres is a well-established, cross-party reality, not an issue exclusive to the Democratic Party. As for this point, we willThe article on the mid-term electionsAs has already been demonstrated, this week’s market has simply taken this on board.

V. NVIDIA: Watchlist for 11–17

On 17 November, NVIDIA released its financial results for the third quarter of the 2027 financial year. This marks the first key milestone following this article that could either confirm or refute the aforementioned assessment, and is therefore worth listing separately.

Let’s first look at the official figures for the previous quarter (Q2 FY2027)

Gross Profit Margin Guidance Both GAAP and non-GAAP are 74.0%, fluctuating by 50 basis points either way

11–17: Four things to look out for

  1. Will total revenue reach the guidance range of US$108 billion (plus or minus 2 per cent), and will data centre revenue continue to account for more than 90 per cent of the total? This is the easiest to quantify and the one on which there is the least disagreement.
  2. Will the non-GAAP gross profit margin hold steady at around 74 per cent? This factor is more important than revenue: if the gross profit margin falls, it indicates that pricing power is waning, and pricing power is the very foundation of this round of valuations.
  3. Has China revised its revenue guidance? Last quarter’s guidance explicitly excluded revenue from computing power at data centres in China. If this revenue is included again this quarter, it would be a signal of a shift in direction—regardless of the amount included, it would indicate a change in the scope of export controls.
  4. Concerns regarding customer concentration and recurring transactions. This is the most difficult question to answer, and also the one most likely to be probed further. If the share of revenue accounted for by the top few customers continues to rise, and if these include entities with investment ties to Nvidia itself, the market will interpret this as ‘demand being artificially inflated’.

It should be noted in all honesty that we have been unable to obtain the specific figures for the market consensus on the financial results for 11–17; therefore, this article does not make any judgements as to whether the results ‘exceeded or fell short of expectations’, but merely lists items for observation. This item will be added following the publication of the financial results.

11–18: Another exam immediately following this one

On the day following the financial results announcement (18 November), Jensen Huang is due to give a closed-door briefing to the Republican chairmen of three House committees regarding his relationship with Megaspeed executive Alice Huang and the company’s counter-espionage security protocols. The three chairmen who jointly requested the briefing are from the Committee on Foreign Affairs (Mast), the Select Committee on China (Moolenaar) and the Committee on Intelligence (Crawford). NVIDIA’s response was that the meeting between Jensen Huang and Alice Huang was no different from meetings with executives from other clients.

This is precisely why this article lists the two dates consecutively.

VI. Anthropic’s pricing: the single most newsworthy event of the next two weeks

If one were to pick a single event over the past fortnight that was ‘most likely to change all valuations without a word being said’, it would not be Nvidia’s earnings report, but Anthropic’s IPO pricing.

First-hand facts (SEC EDGAR): Anthropic’s S-1/A-1 was filed on 2 November 2026Submitted. However, the cover sheet still bears the notation ‘Subject to Completion’,Both the ‘Price Range’ and ‘Number of Shares Issued’ fields have been left blank. This means that the pricing range will appear in subsequent amendments to the S-1/A filing, rather than in this version.

Timetable: The roadshow is set to begin as early as 10 November (as reported by Yahoo), and the target of listing before Thanksgiving remains unchanged.

Valuation range (all figures are media estimates and do not represent the company’s official figures): The *Wall Street Journal* reported that the range under discussion for the valuation was between 1.5 trillion and 2 trillion US dollars ($1.5T–$2.0T); On 1 October, Bloomberg cited a reasonable valuation from the investor side of between 1.8 trillion and 2 trillion US dollars ($1.8T–$2.0T).Neither of these figures was released by Anthropic or the underwriters; they are labelled as media estimates.

Why does it contain more information than Nvidia’s financial results?: Nvidia’s financial results confirm existing demand, whilst Anthropic’s pricing represents a public auction addressing the question of ‘just how much are AI assets actually worth?’.If the price rises to the upper end of the range, indicating that the primary market’s bids for AI assets have not been reduced as a result of the 5 November event, then the decline in the secondary market represents a localised revaluation; If the price settles at the lower end of the range or even falls below it, then the 5 November event is not an isolated incident, but rather the start of a broader revaluation.

VII. Scenario Iteration: From 45/40/15 to 40/45/15

We are in‘In the same week, macro and micro perspectives delivered conflicting verdicts on AI capital expenditure’The three scenarios provided areContinue 45 per cent / Slow down 40 per cent / Break 15 per cent. Over the course of this week, we have fine-tuned them as follows:

Scenario Old probability New Probability Trigger Conditions and Monitoring Indicators
Continuation: Capital expenditure continues, with growth remaining steady 45% 40% 11–17: NVIDIA’s data centre revenue met expectations and gross margin held steady at 74 per cent; Anthropic’s pricing came in at the upper end of the range; credit spreads ceased to widen following the December interest rate hike
slow down: Growth has slowed, but the absolute figure has not decreased 40% 45% Substantial delays in obtaining state-level licences; financing costs for neocloud companies such as CoreWeave continue to rise; capital expenditure guidance for 2027 has been revised downwards but remains positive
Rupture: A decline in the absolute amount of capital expenditure 15% 15% Neocloud defaults or undergoes debt restructuring; a major player publicly cuts its absolute capital expenditure for 2027; Nvidia revises its guidance downwards for the second consecutive quarter

There are only three reasons for the change

  1. Political constraints have been quantified for the first time. Before the figures 45 per cent and 40 per cent were introduced, ‘political risk’ was a qualitative description that could not be incorporated into the model; once they were introduced, it became a growth rate assumption that could be revised downwards.
  2. The credit market has already priced this in. Before the stock market’s catch-up fall on 5 November, CDS spreads and bond yields had already been trending downwards for a month. The credit market is usually an earlier and more accurate indicator than the stock market, and this occasion was no exception.
  3. The pricing in of an 80 per cent interest rate rise has eliminated the buffer provided by low-cost funding. In addition to the first two reasons, this one completely rules out the option of ‘just gritting your teeth and getting through it’.

Let us be clear: the slowdown relates to new growth, not existing levels.

The ‘slowdown’ scenario does not imply that any company will reduce the investment it has already committed to. This refers to: a slower pace of commencement for new projects, less favourable pricing terms for new orders, and incremental capital expenditure in 2027 being lower than previously guided. Data centres already under construction will not be halted as a result.

If NVIDIA’s results on 11–17 exceed expectations, or if Anthropic’s valuation rises to the upper end of the range, we will adjust the probabilities to 45/40/15. Either of these two events is sufficient to demonstrate that we have overestimated the speed at which political factors take effect.

IX. Explanation of Data Gaps and Definitions

There are several gaps in this article that must be clearly indicated; readers are advised to bear this in mind when using the data contained herein:

  2. 11-06: The closing price for that day was not recorded. This article only records the closing figures for 5 November and the changes in the probability of an interest rate rise on 6 November; it does not include the specific closing levels of the three major indices on 6 November.
  3. As regards the discrepancy between Goldman Sachs’ 45 per cent and Bloomberg’s 40 per cent in their calculation methods, we are only aware of the information reported by the media.   4. ‘Retail investors sold off US$358 million in a single day’, ‘VIX at 24.23’ and ‘the worst since 1896’ – all three are second-hand accounts.(Source: Yahoo Finance and The Wall Street Journal’s intraday reports); no first-hand statistics were obtained.
