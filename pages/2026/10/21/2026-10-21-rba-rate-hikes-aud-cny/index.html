Australia Rate Hikes and the AUD/CNY Exchange Rate: RBA 2026 Tightening Cycle and AUD/CNY Scenario Analysis

In 2026, the Reserve Bank of Australia (RBA) raised the cash rate from 3.60 per cent to 4.60 per cent, returning it to its highest level since 2011. How will this round of monetary tightening affect Australian households, businesses and tenants? With the interest rate differential between China and Australia now standing at 3.6 percentage points, where is the AUD/CNY exchange rate headed? This article brings together an analysis of the RBA’s rate-hiking cycle, macroeconomic and social impacts, global interest rate differentials and multi-scenario analysis of the AUD/CNY exchange rate, concluding with recommended timeframes for currency exchange and key indicators to monitor.

This article is based on personal research and analysis; the data is current as at 20 October 2026. The probabilities of the scenarios are based on subjective judgement and do not constitute any investment or foreign exchange advice.

NOTES

Key Findings

  1. Nature of the interest rate hike cycle: The RBA raised interest rates by a cumulative 100 basis points during 2026 (with the cash rate rising from 3.60 per cent to 4.60 per cent, the most recent increase taking place on 30 September 2026); the primary focus of this round of rate rises was to ‘curb inflation’ rather than to ‘prevent overheating’ . The RBA has maintained a hawkish stance, and the market has already priced in a further increase of approximately 15 basis points by the end of the year, followed by a further increase of approximately 30 basis points in the first half of 2027.
  2. Economic and Social Polarisation:
    • The economic landscape is showing a rare ‘scissor gap’: Mortgage repayments account for around 10 per cent of household disposable income, and consumption is gradually slowing; house prices have fallen by 1.6 per cent from their March peak (with investor loans leading the decline); however, on the corporate side, driven by AI and data centre construction, private investment has bucked the trend and grown by over 10 per cent.
    • Highly uneven distribution of social pressure: The pressure is concentrated onNew borrowers with high leveragewithLow-income tenants; the unemployment rate rose from 3.5 per cent to 4.6 per cent (a five-year high, including the impact of adjustments to the statistical methodology).
  3. Global Interest Rates and Yield Spreads:
    • On 17 September 2026, the Federal Reserve also raised interest rates by 25 basis points to 3.75 per cent–4.00 per cent, thereby disproving the assumption that the RBA was the ‘sole hawk’.
    • The spread on the 10-year long end of the yield curve is extremely wide: the Australian 10-year bond (5.23 per cent–5.26 per cent) is significantly higher than the Chinese 10-year bond (1.64 per cent), with the China–Australia spread at approximately +3.6pp; the 10-year yield spread between China and the US is approximately +3.5pp(US 10-year Treasury bonds: 5.11 per cent–5.12 per cent).
    • Policy rates: RBA 4.60% vs China’s 1-year LPR 3.0% (spread +1.6 percentage points). The long-end yield spread is supporting the Australian dollar, but this has already been fully priced into the current exchange rate.
  4. AUD/CNY Scenario Analysis:
    • The AUD/CNY exchange rate currently stands at approximately 4.6647 (RBA official figure, 20 October 2026), constrained by the interest rate differential between China and Australia, the moderate appreciation of the renminbi against the US dollar (USD/CNY 6.6861) and the counter-trend decline in iron ore prices.
    • Short-term (3–6 months) benchmark range: 4.50–4.75 (probability 52 per cent), with a slight bias towards weakness; Probability of a downtrend: 28 per cent; probability of an uptrend: 20 per cent.
    • Medium-term (1–2 years) benchmark range: 4.40–4.80 (50 per cent probability), with a tendency towards weakness amidst volatility; The key turning point will be when the RBA’s interest rates peak and begin to fall in 2027.
  5. Currency exchange guide: There is currently no clear one-way trend. The conditions for buying Australian dollars (for study abroad, rent and living expenses) are gradually improving, and converting them back into renminbi is relatively favourable in the short term; keep a close eye on the CPI figures at the end of October, the RBA’s decision in December and the trend in iron ore prices.

I. A Review of the RBA’s 2026 Interest Rate Hike Cycle

1.1 The path of rising cash rates

In 2026, the RBA reversed the cycle of interest rate cuts seen in 2025 (three cuts totalling 75 basis points to 3.60 per cent in 2025) and raised interest rates cumulatively during the year. 100bp, with the cash rate surpassing the peak of the previous tightening cycle (4.35 per cent from November 2023 to 2024) to reach its highest level since 2011.

1.2 Policy rationale: curbing inflation, rather than preventing overheating

Pursuant to the announcement of the Board resolution dated 29 September 2026 (MR-26-27), this round of contraction is driven by a combination of external supply shocks and domestic structural rigidities:

  • External inflation: Geopolitical tensions in the Middle East continue to escalate, with global energy and shipping prices significantly higher than the RBA’s previous forecasts.
  • Technological demands drive up costs: Global AI computing power and infrastructure development have driven up the prices of technology hardware, electricity infrastructure and specialist equipment.
  • Domestic production capacity bottlenecks: The labour supply and construction capacity have long been in a delicate balance.
  • Official statement: Although the previous interest rate rises have led to a marked slowdown in economic activity, trimmed-mean inflation remains well above the 2 per cent–3 per cent target range; the Board considers that ‘the risks of inaction far outweigh the risks of tightening’.

1.3 Macroeconomic Expectations and Market Pricing

Based on the RBA’s August Monetary Policy Statement (SMP) and Minutes of the Board Meeting of 29 September：

  • Inflation: Year-on-year overall CPI for the quarter ending June 2026 3.9%, Trimmed Mean CPI year-on-year 3.6%. The RBA forecasts that inflation will not peak until Early 2028 only then will it return to the 2.5 per cent benchmark.
  • Growth and Employment: GDP growth is expected to slow to by the end of 2026 1.4%; the unemployment rate is forecast to rise to in 2028 4.8%。
  • Market pricing: As of October 2026, interest rate derivatives had already factored in the September rate rise and priced it in A further increase of approximately 15 basis points by the end of 2026、A further increase of approximately 30 basis points in the first half of 2027。

II. The Impact of Interest Rate Rises on the Australian Economy

The transmission of interest rate rises is characterised by a multi-track pattern, with ‘households under pressure, businesses resilient and external demand showing mixed trends’.

2.1 Household finances: the debt burden is approaching its peak

  • High debt-servicing ratios: The total amount of scheduled principal and interest repayments has risen to approximately 10%, approaching its peak since 2024.
  • Consumer spending is slowing but remains resilient: Growth in retail sales continues to slow. However, since the start of 2024, real per capita disposable income has shown a modest recovery, and homeowners who purchased property earlier have built up substantial buffers for mortgage repayments (Offset/Redraw accounts) during the period of low interest rates. Whilst overall consumption is weaker than in previous years, it remains stronger than the levels suggested by confidence indicators.

2.2 Property: Demand under pressure, whilst supply remains inflexible

  • Price correction: The All-Australia House Price Index has fallen cumulatively since its peak in March 2026 1.6%, slightly exceeding the RBA’s initial estimates.
  • Credit tightening: A decline in borrowing capacity has led to a marked reduction in new housing loans, withInvestor loans led the decline… coupled with market expectations of policy changes regarding negative gearing and capital gains tax (CGT).
  • The supply-demand gap has not disappeared: The RBA has repeatedly emphasised that net migration and planning restrictions continue to underpin housing demand; whilst interest rate rises have primarily curbed purchasing power and transaction volumes, they have not eliminated the supply-demand gap.

2.3 The corporate sector: the ‘scissors effect’ resulting from AI investment

  • In contrast to the household sector, investment by Australia’s private non-financial enterprises bucked the trend and grew year-on-yearMore than 10 per cent。
  • The main source of power comes fromConstruction of large-scale data centres, AI computing hubs and supporting renewable energy grids. The boom in corporate capital expenditure has largely offset the drag on GDP caused by the contraction in consumption.

2.4 Trade and Exchange Rate Transmission

  • Currency appreciation: The Australian dollar’s trade-weighted index (TWI) has appreciated by approximately 5%(Latest: 64.7), which has helped to offset some of the imported inflation.
  • Product differentiation: The RBA’s Commodity Price Index rose year-on-year on 1 October 15.5%(denominated in SDRs), but there are marked internal differences:Iron ore, alumina and copper ore bucked the trend and fell, whilst other commodities generally rose. The weakness in iron ore—which accounts for the largest share of exports to China—is a structural risk weighing on Australia’s terms of trade.

2.5 Resilience of the financial system

According to the RBA’s October 2026 Financial Stability Review (FSR),MR-26-28）：

  • The banking system’s capital adequacy ratio and liquidity buffer remain at prudently high levels.
  • Mortgage arrears rates remain under control; the proportion of owner-occupiers experiencing a cash flow shortfallLess than 2 per cent… the proportion of loans with negative equity is very low, and there is currently no risk of systemic mortgage defaults.

III. Social Polarisation Resulting from Interest Rate Rises

The costs of this round of austerity have not been borne equally across society, but have instead been distributed in a markedly asymmetrical manner.

3.1 Existing property owners and new borrowers

  • Long-standing property owners (with strong resilience): Households that purchased a property during the period of low interest rates between 2020 and 2022, or which have accumulated a substantial offset balance, have experienced a relatively minor net impact from the interest rate rises; some households have even realised a net interest gain as deposit rates have risen to between 4 per cent and 5 per cent.
  • New borrowers (highly vulnerable): Young households that entered the property market at peak prices over the past 1–3 years, with high debt-to-income (DTI) ratios and thin savings buffers, have borne the brunt of the impact. However, RBA survey data shows that even among the lowest-income quintile of borrowers, the median savings balance is still sufficient to cover nearly a year’s worth of repayments.

3.2 Low-income tenants: the dual pressures of high inflation and high rents

  • The RBA’s September minutes clearly state that,Low-income tenantsThey are the most vulnerable group in this current cycle of inflation and monetary tightening.
  • They have neither property to hedge against inflation nor much return on high-interest savings; meanwhile, the shortage of housing means landlords continue to pass on their holding costs, with rents rising significantly faster than wages. RBA Governor Bullock has stated that high inflation is essentially a regressive tax; the turmoil in the Middle East is making the whole of Australia poorer, with the most vulnerable bearing the brunt of it.

3.3 The Labour Market: A Rise in the Unemployment Rate and Changes in Statistical Methodology

  • The unemployment rate has fallen from the start of this cycle’s 3.5% Rise to 4.6%(Figures published in August).
  • Explanation of terms: The RBA noted that the unemployment rate jumped from 4.4 per cent in June to 4.6 per cent, partly due to a technical rise resulting from the sample rotation and methodological adjustments in the Australian Bureau of Statistics’ (ABS) Labour Force Survey (LFS), which should be interpreted with caution; however, the trend towards a marginally looser labour market has been confirmed.

Interest rate differentials are a key benchmark for assessing the medium-term pricing of non-US currencies. The following data is current as at 20 October 2026.

  • China 10Y: 1.64%(TradingEconomics interbank quotes; its report of 15 October stated 1.67 per cent). (Confidence level: medium)

  3. Policy interest rates: RBA 4.60% versus LPR 3.0%, spread +1.6 percentage points; Fed 3.75%–4.00% versus LPR, spread +0.75 to +1.0 percentage points.

4.3 Spillover effects of the Federal Reserve’s shift to interest rate rises

  • Key assumptions have been refuted: The Federal Reserve on 2026-09-17 Interest rates were raised by 25 basis points to 3.75%–4.00%(Source:Fed OMO), bringing the cycle of interest rate cuts in 2025 to an end.
  • The meaning of ‘cross-market’: The narrative of the “RBA going it alone on a hawkish path” no longer holds water, as the global economy has returned to an environment of synchronised monetary tightening in the US and Australia. The Fed’s shift towards a hawkish stance has bolstered the US dollar (with the broad US Dollar Index stabilising around 121.40), capping the upside potential for the Australian dollar against the US dollar; however, the relatively strong US dollar is also putting pressure on the renminbi, with the two factors partially offsetting each other in the AUD/CNY exchange rate.

4.4 The marginal effect of interest rate differentials on exchange rates

The China–Australia interest rate differential is a long-term structural factor that supports the Australian dollar and weighs on the renminbi. However, the widening of the long-end yield spread has persisted for several months, and this factorThis has already been fully priced in at the current exchange rate of around 4.6647. The interest rate differential acts more as a ‘safety net’ to prevent a sharp fall in the Australian dollar than as a new catalyst to drive the exchange rate to continue rising in a one-way direction.

5.1 Present Value of Exchange Rates and Cross-Validation

  • Spot exchange rate (20 October 2026, official RBA figure):
    • AUD/CNY = 4.6647(Over the past three days: 4.6469 → 4.6607 → 4.6647, a slight rebound)
    • AUD/USD = 0.6984, Australian Dollar TWI = 64.7
  • Cross-check: The Federal Reserve’s H.10 data was released at the same time USD/CNY = 6.6861。

The theoretical figure differs by approximately 0.1 per cent from the 4.6647 published by the RBA; the data is reliable.

5.2 Key driving variables

  1. China-Australia interest rate differential (bullish for AUD/CNY, but has lost its edge): The +3.6pp spread at the long end is limiting the downside, but its marginal impact is waning.
  2. Commodities (bearish): Iron ore prices are bucking the trend and falling. As the key link between Chinese demand and Australian export revenues, this weakness is directly weighing on the Australian dollar.
  3. Renminbi exchange rate (bearish on AUD/CNY): According to the Federal Reserve’s H.10 data, the USD/CNY exchange rate fell from 6.6937 on 13 October to 6.6861 on 16 October; the renminbi’s steady appreciation against the US dollar has limited the upside potential for the AUD/CNY exchange rate.
  4. Risk premium (bearish): The spread of hostilities in the Middle East has fuelled demand for safe-haven assets; as a risk-sensitive currency, the Australian dollar is prone to coming under pressure when capital flows back into the US dollar.

VI. Foreign Exchange Reference Points and Key Monitoring Indicators

6.1 Currency Exchange Guide

  • Need to buy Australian dollars (for tuition fees, rent, living expenses and essential expenses upon arrival in Australia): There is no need to chase higher prices. The China-Australia interest rate differential has already been fully priced in, and with the renminbi strengthening moderately against the US dollar, there is limited upside above 4.66 in the short term. Should geopolitical shocks cause the Australian dollar to pull back to 4.50–4.55… you might consider purchasing foreign currency in batches as and when required. In the medium term, the RBA’s policy shift in 2027 may result in lower currency exchange costs.
  • Requirement to convert RMB (remittance of income from Australia, trade settlement): We are currently in a period of protection against high interest rate differentials,The 4.65–4.75 range is a relatively favourable window for foreign exchange conversion. Given that the interest rate differential advantage is likely to gradually diminish by 2027, it is not advisable to allocate assets in Australian dollars based on expectations of a one-way appreciation.

6.2 Checklist for Monitoring Key Indicators

  1. RBA Interest Rate Decision and Minutes: Pay particular attention to the wording of the resolution on 1–2 December 2026, including forward guidance on the scale of interest rate rises in 2027, and whether there are any signs of a shift from a hawkish to a dovish stance.
  2. Inflation figures: The CPI figures for the third quarter of 2026, released at the end of October, and in particularTruncated mean CPI Will there be a clear decline from 3.6 per cent towards 3.0 per cent? This is the decisive factor in whether the RBA will raise interest rates for the fifth time in December.
  3. Long-term interest rates and policy in China: China Bond 10-year valuation and whether there have been any changes to the 1-year and 5-year LPRs (more precise valuations can be verified on the China Bond website at a later date).
  4. Commodities: The Singapore Exchange (SGX) iron ore swap benchmark contract and the Platts 62% Index, to gauge whether iron ore prices have bottomed out and stabilised.
  5. The Australian labour market: The unemployment rate in the ABS Monthly Labour Force Survey: the threshold is 4.8 per cent; monitor whether the rise is accelerating.
