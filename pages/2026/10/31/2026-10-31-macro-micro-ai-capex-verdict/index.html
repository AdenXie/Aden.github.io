In the same week, macro and micro perspectives delivered conflicting assessments of AI capital expenditure

Introduction: At our meeting on 24 October,‘AI: The Mainstay of Capital Expenditure’In a previous article, we analysed in depth how the tech giants’ investments of hundreds of billions of dollars in computing power single-handedly prop up half of the US economy; subsequently, in*The Year AI Agents Went Haywire*In this piece, we explored the dark side of governance amidst the rapid advance of cutting-edge technologies. However, in the final days of late October 2026, the global financial markets were the scene of a highly dramatic ‘split’:At the macro level, the US Bureau of Economic Analysis (BEA) and the Federal Reserve (Fed) have concluded that ‘the economy is exceptionally strong’.—Real GDP in the third quarter jumped to an annualised rate of +3.4%, and the Federal Reserve even went so far as to explicitly state in its policy statement—in black and white—that ‘productivity is strong and capital investment is robust’;However, at the micro level, the tech giants on the secondary market have faced a brutal, storm-like reckoning.—Alphabet plummeted by 12.6 per cent in a single day (according to StockTitan, marking its largest single-day fall since its IPO in 2004), with its market capitalisation evaporating by approximately US$500 billion; Amazon suffered a sharp after-hours slump due to capital expenditure of 100 billion and deteriorating free cash flow, with major media outlets exclaiming that “a trillion dollars in market capitalisation has evaporated”. The macro picture is celebrating, whilst the micro picture is bleeding. Why has the same AI-driven boom received such diametrically opposed verdicts within the same week?

Prologue: The Divided Pendulum and the Illusory Consensus

The week of 28 to 30 October 2026 is destined to go down in the history of fintech as a ‘week of misalignment’ that will be analysed time and again.

If one were to look only at the headlines of macroeconomic news, everyone would conclude that ‘the US economy is enjoying a golden age’:

  • Real GDP growth exceeds three per cent: The preliminary figure for US real GDP in the third quarter, released on 29 October, showed an annualised quarter-on-quarter increase of +3.4%, marking a sharp acceleration compared with the +2.2 per cent recorded in the second quarter;
  • Endorsement from the highest levels of the government: In its statement following the monetary policy meeting on 28 October, the Federal Reserve explicitly characterised the macroeconomy as ‘expanding at a strong pace’ and specifically endorsed ‘strong productivity growth and robust capital investment’.

However, on the trading floor of Wall Street the previous night, an indiscriminate valuation bloodbath awaited the tech giants:

  • Alphabet Although cloud business revenue more than doubled, the share price plummeted on 29 October as search revenue fell slightly short of expectations and free cash flow remained negative for the second consecutive quarter. 12.6%(According to StockTitan statistics, this marked the largest single-day fall in the company’s history since its listing in 2004), wiping out approximately US$500 billion；
  • Amazon Following hot on its heels, the company released its financial results; although its cloud computing division, AWS, recorded its fastest growth in nearly five years (+39.3 per cent), it suffered a severe cash drain due to maintaining capital expenditure of as much as US$220 billion for 2026, leading to a sharp fall in after-hours trading. 5 per cent to 8 per cent；
  • Panic spread rapidly, and on 30 October (the last trading day of the month), the Nasdaq Composite Index closed lower 2.86% to 25,437 points; the S&P 500 closed lower 1.82% to 7,580.07 points, with both posting their worst single-day performance since June.

Many financial media outlets have simplistically and bluntly attributed this development to the ‘bursting of the AI bubble’.

But if you look beyond the underlying ledger, you will arrive at a completely different, yet more profound conclusion:

Neither the macro nor the micro perspective is wrong; they simply offer different perspectives on this capital expenditure—the largest in human history—at the same moment in time.

I. Understanding the current ‘divergence in judgements’ in three sentences

Before undertaking a detailed analysis of the official data, we can first distil the essence of this misalignment into three key facts:

  1. In the grand scheme of things, AI isn’t quite as mysterious as people imagine：
    Of the 3.4 per cent GDP growth in the third quarter, the gross contribution from investment in capital goods—primarily information processing equipment—was only approximately 1.16 percentage points (accounting for approximately 34 per cent of the growth, and this represents the upper limit of AI’s contribution); more importantly, the original BEA text explicitly states that this batch of computing hardware is ‘primarily based on imported data’—The money comes from US tech giants, but a large proportion of the added value remains in the overseas chip manufacturing locations., after deducting the reduction in imports, the net boost to the US economy is far lower than the widely circulated claim that it accounts for ‘more than half’.
  2. What actually drove growth from 2.2 per cent to 3.4 per cent were two factors entirely unrelated to AI：
    Consumption (+2.52 percentage points) remains the largest component, whilst the main driver of the acceleration in the third quarter compared with the second quarter was, in fact,A significant restocking of private inventories (contributing +1.61 percentage points)withExpansion of federal government expenditure (contribution: +0.90 percentage points). This round of acceleration is essentially a combination of a traditional inventory cycle rebound and a fiscal stimulus.
  3. The market has never been concerned with whether there is ‘demand for AI’, but rather with ‘when this investment will generate cash flow’.：
    Google Cloud’s doubling (+103 per cent) and AWS’s fastest growth in five years (+39.3 per cent) provide irrefutable evidence that demand for AI is exceptionally strong; The reason the stock market has dealt such a heavy blow is to punish the tech giants for their reckless over-spending, which has “burned through the quarter’s free cash flow to a massive negative figure simply to secure contracts”.The equity market is pricing in which cash flows will run out first, whilst the bond market is pricing in that the 3.4 per cent growth floor has not been breached.。

A Substantive Revision to Our Previous Argument on ‘Load-bearing Columns’

In our article ‘AI Capital Expenditure: The Mainstay’, published on 24 October, we noted that Bridgewater estimated AI capital expenditure would contribute approximately 1.4 percentage points to full-year 2026 growth (accounting for half of that growth), and pointed out that the preliminary third-quarter GDP figures due on 29 October would serve as a stress test for the extent to which AI drives growth.

Today, the official preliminary figures have provided the final verdict:

  1. Mao’s contribution accounted for only 34 per cent：
    Of the 3.4 per cent overall growth, investment in equipment—primarily information processing equipment—contributed +1.16 percentage points(1.16 ÷ 3.40 ≈ 34 per cent). This figure falls far short of the widely publicised claim that ‘AI alone accounted for over half the growth’, and is certainly not comparable to the ‘74 per cent’ recorded in the first quarter under the aggressive algorithm;
  2. The growth in equipment “is mainly attributable to imports”：
    In the official text of its press release, BEA specifically stated in black and white: the surge in investment in computers and peripheral equipment,“Based primarily on import data”。
    In macroeconomic national accounts, high-end chips and assembled servers procured from TSMC or overseas are first included once in ‘domestic fixed investment’ (+1.16 pp), but are then immediately deducted by the same amount from ‘total imports’ (–2.64 pp; capital goods account for the lion’s share of import deductions).
    The conclusion is clear and stark: the vast majority of the money spent by these giants has gone towards the profits and output of overseas supply chains, whilst the net value added that actually contributes to the US domestic GDP is extremely meagre.
  3. The key lies in the micro level, not in the marginal change in GDP：
    This calculation constitutes a significant revision to our previous statement:AI capital expenditure is indeed an absolute mainstay of the balance sheets of companies such as Microsoft and Amazon, but in terms of the marginal net increase in US GDP, it is not as indispensable as is often claimed.

The September shortfall masked by the quarterly average

Third-quarter GDP figures appear to paint a rosy picture, even bringing the annualised increase in the PCE price index down from 5.0 per cent in the second quarter to 2.1%(Core PCE stood at 2.5 per cent). However, a breakdown of the high-frequency data for September alone reveals that the real economy was already showing signs of strain at the end of the quarter:

  • Prices have edged up month-on-month: The price index for personal consumption expenditure rose month-on-month in September 0.5%, rebounding year-on-year to 3.6%(Core year-on-year: 3.1 per cent);
  • The real drivers of consumption have stalled: In September, real personal consumption expenditure, adjusted for inflation, rose by a mere US$2.6 billion month-on-month, remaining almost atzero-growth state；
  • Residents’ water tanks have run dry: Real personal disposable income fell month-on-month -0.3%, the personal savings rate has fallen to 3.8% a historic low.

A consumer economy fuelled by drawing down savings has, coupled with monthly inflation resurging at the end of the quarter, forced the Federal Reserve into an unprecedented institutional impasse.

III. The Federal Reserve’s Statement: Strong Official Endorsement, Yet a Split Among Voting Members

On 28 October, the day before the GDP figures were released, the Federal Reserve concluded its two-day FOMC monetary policy meeting. The final decision was to maintain the target range for the federal funds rate at 3.75%–4.00% Unchanged (following a 25-basis-point rate rise in September).

However, behind the calm tone regarding the base rate, the entire official statement was rife with an extremely rare exchange of sharp retorts:

1. Three strong statements in the resolution’s preamble

In the body of the statement, the Federal Reserve, in a rare move, employed highly definitive language:

  • “Economic activity is expanding at a strong pace”— has completely eliminated the risk of a short-term recession;
  • “Productivity growth is strong, and capital investment remains robust”— The Federal Reserve has officially characterised the development of AI infrastructure as a core, positive force underpinning the economy in its official statement;
  • Yet immediately afterwards, he still insisted:“Inflation remains elevated”。

2. A 10-to-2 split vote and undercurrents regarding interest rate rises

At the final vote, the Federal Reserve was split into two camps:

Federal Reserve Governor Lisa Cook and Beth Hammack, President of the Cleveland Federal Reserve Bank Both cast votes against the decision, with both voting members explicitly advocating for a further interest rate rise of 25 basis points at that meeting.。

At the post-meeting press conference, the new Chair of the Federal Reserve, Kevin Warsh, demonstrated an extremely skilful balancing act:

On the one hand, he reassured the markets by stating that the macroeconomy was ‘pointing in the right direction’, that he did not believe there was currently any uncontrollable overheating, and that the Federal Reserve’s role ‘is not to throw sand in the gears’ of the economic engine;

However, on the other hand, upon closer examination of his remarks and the broader context, this journal’s analysis suggests that the underlying message is extremely clear:With GDP growth at 3.4 per cent, AI investment in full swing and September’s inflation rebounding to 3.6 per cent, the Federal Reserve has no grounds whatsoever to support a rate cut, yet there are at least two compelling reasons to resume rate rises in December.

Just one day after the meeting (30 October), two more Federal Reserve members publicly stated that ‘interest rates need to be higher’ (as recorded in Yahoo Finance’s live closing market coverage) — the division has spread from the voting benches in the boardroom to the public arena.

AI capital expenditure is becoming the Federal Reserve’s most vexing underlying variable—it not only provides growth at the macro level that prevents an economic downturn, but also competes for electricity and commodities at the physical level, thereby firmly blocking the path to interest rate cuts.

IV. Financial Results Season Review: Four Major Companies, Four Distinctly Different Causes

If macroeconomic data suggests that capital continues to flow steadily into data centres, why did secondary market investors unleash a wave of selling targeting tech blue-chips on 28–29 October?

To dismiss this round of market turbulence as merely the ‘bursting of the AI bubble’ is the laziest approach one can take in technology reporting. In fact, a closer examination of the four sets of financial statements released during this round reveals that the four tech giants are facing four entirely distinct underlying issues:

  • The true cause of the illness: A precipitous drop in net profit and cash flow. The company reported earnings per share (EPS) of US$2.12 (significantly below the expected US$3.01; CNBC reported an adjusted figure of US$2.67, with the discrepancy primarily attributable to substantial non-operating book losses on equity investments); What shocked long-term investors even more was that, in a single quarter,Free cash flow (FCF) stood at US$9.07 billion, having deteriorated further following its first-ever dip into negative territory in the second quarter。

2. Amazon: Uncontrolled spending + free cash flow continues to take a heavy hit

  • Business Update: Total revenue amounted to US$201.9 billion ($201.9B), up 12 per cent year-on-year, slightly below Wall Street’s expectations; AWS, the cloud computing leader, saw its revenue surge year-on-year +39.3 per cent to US$46 billion, marking the highest growth rate in the past five years; CEO Andy Jassy even predicted during the conference call that AWS’s growth is expected to consistently exceed the 40 per cent mark between 2027 and 2028; operating profit reached a record high of US$28.1 billion (with a profit margin of 13.9 per cent);
  • The true cause of the illness: Astronomical capital expenditure is causing cash to drain away. This marks Amazon’s second foray into the debt market this year: in June, it secured a $17.5 billion credit facility (Reuters). As Bloomberg put it, this is the latest move to “maintain financial flexibility” amidst hundreds of billions of dollars in AI spending;In other words, once their free cash flow has run dry, the tech giants begin to use money from the debt market to keep their computing power afloat.。
  • Market Verdict: Sharp post-market fall 5 per cent to 8 per cent. The logic of Wall Street is utterly ruthless—even though AWS has posted its fastest growth in five years, investors find it hard to accept that all the hard-earned cash generated by the entire company is being poured into data centres and a computing power black hole, and that the company is even having to fork out an additional $40 billion or more in liquidity.

Although, from an analytical perspective, this revenue of ten billion includes nested revenue flows arising from OpenAI’s procurement of Azure cloud computing power and revenue-sharing arrangements, it has successfully demonstrated to the market that ‘Microsoft is currently the only tech giant capable of converting computing expenditure into direct profit at a double-digit rate’;
  • Market Verdict: Share price rose against the market trend following the release of the financial results +4.0%。

4. Intel: A casualty of the traditional business cycle – a grim outlook unrelated to AI

  • Business Update: Intel’s guidance for fourth-quarter gross profit margin has fallen sharply compared with the third quarter 6.1 percentage points, management has issued a clear warning that traditional business operations are under pressure;
  • Market Verdict: It closed at US$92.07 on 29 October, before plummeting in after-hours trading -23.0%; The following day (30 October), the panic in the markets continued, with shares closing sharply lower -28.6% to around US$65.8, marking its largest single-day fall since 1974 (Yahoo Finance). This is essentially a double blow resulting from the bottoming out of the traditional consumer electronics cycle and depreciation in contract manufacturing; attributing it solely to AI capital expenditure is a complete misinterpretation at the industry level.

V. Market Structure: Who is setting the price for growth, and who is setting the price for cash flow?

Amidst this earnings season storm, the financial markets have exhibited a textbook example of a split in sentiment:

  1. Precision Targeting of Technology Growth Stocks：
    On 29 October, the Nasdaq Composite fell by 1.38 per cent, whilst the S&P 500 edged down by 0.31 per cent; by 30 October, the final trading day of the month, the sell-off reached its peak: the Nasdaq Composite closed lower 2.86% to 25,437 points; the S&P 500 closed lower 1.82% Reaching 7,580.07 points, both indices recorded their worst single-day performance since June, with the Nasdaq having fallen by around 8 per cent from its recent high (CNBC). The widely circulated claim that “tech stocks lost a trillion dollars in market capitalisation in a single day” is essentially a media estimate, but the core of the story is highly accurate—The sell-off has been concentrated specifically on heavyweight market leaders facing massive capital expenditure and deteriorating free cash flow (Alphabet alone accounts for 500 billion).；
  2. Safe-haven capital is flooding into the government bond market：
    In stark contrast to the turmoil in the stock market, the US Treasury market saw strong buying support during the same week. The yield on the benchmark 10-year US Treasury note fell to its lowest level in nearly two months.

These diametrically opposed capital flows reveal the true pricing models currently employed by professional institutions:

  • Stock traders are pricing in the ‘risk of a disruption to micro-cash flows’: When tech giants are willing to pour as much as US$40 billion of their free cash flow into the race for computing power, big-tech shares—which were once regarded as defensive assets—lose their cash flow safety cushion and are downgraded to high-risk investments;
  • Bond traders are pricing in ‘a solid foundation for GDP growth of 3.4 per cent’: Regardless of whether the tech giants make a profit this quarter, as long as they cash their 200 billion Capex cheques, the entire real economy and construction supply chain will continue to function, and there is absolutely no risk of a cliff-edge recession in the short term.

VI. Historical Comparison: The Chronological Reversal of the Telecoms Fibre-Optics Bubble of the 1990s

In our earlier macroeconomic research, we have on several occasions drawn parallels between the current expansion of AI data centres and the global telecoms fibre-optic boom of 1996 to 2001: back then, the global industry spent Approximately 2 trillion US dollars ($2T), laid more than 80 million miles of advanced fibre-optic cable, with telecoms capital expenditure accounting for up to 1.0%–1.2%, but four years after the bubble burst, there are still Between 85 per cent and 95 per cent of optical cables end up as dark fibre that has never carried any light.。

However, having reviewed this week’s market movements, we can distil a highly forward-looking macroeconomic hypothesis—The timing of the correction in this AI cycle is likely to be the exact opposite of that in the 1990s：

Timeline of the Liquidation Process Micro trends peak first ➔ Macro trends confirm later：
1. Nasdaq technology shares were the first to peak and plummet in March 2000;
2. It was only in the following quarters that telecoms expenditure gradually began to decline and eventually peaked;
3. It was not until after 2001 that the macroeconomic GDP recession was officially recognised in the statistics.		 Confirm the macro picture first ➔ Settle the micro details first：
1. Macroeconomic data was the first to confirm spending (GDP for Q3 2026 recorded growth of 3.4 per cent);
2. During the earnings season, the micro-level stock market was the first to react to the deterioration in free cash flow;
3. Macroeconomic value added is not fully retained within the country due to import leakage.		 ⚠️ Macroeconomic indicators lag behind expenditure, whilst the stock market leads returns。
The macro view records ‘how money has been spent over the past three months’, whilst the micro view scrutines ‘whether the money can be recouped over the next three years’.

The most critical reality lies in the intergenerational differences in the depreciation patterns of assets:

Even though the fibre-optic cables buried in the ground all those years ago had lain dormant for eight years, the glass fibres remained intact and were ultimately snapped up by Netflix and YouTube as the foundation for the streaming boom;

But the millions of advanced GPUs currently piled up in the server room,Within three years, depreciation costs will be ruthlessly reflected in the financial statements, and within five years, the equipment will face physical obsolescence in terms of energy efficiency.. The tech giants have not had the luxury of waiting a full decade for demand to develop naturally; every day, they are racing against the physical ageing of silicon wafers.

VIII. Honest Explanation of Data Definitions and Factual Boundaries

When reading this report, readers are asked to maintain a clear understanding of the boundaries:

  1. The accuracy of AI-driven investment contributions: In the official BEA press release, ‘information processing equipment’ is listed as the main sub-category driving investment in equipment (+1.16 pp); however, due to delays in format consolidation of the NIPA’s comprehensive multi-dimensional interactive data tables during the initial release of preliminary figures, the 1.16 percentage points represents the upper limit of the contribution to overall equipment investment; the precise contribution attributable solely to AI computing power, once isolated, may be slightly lower;
  2. Regarding media reports of ‘a trillion dollars wiped off in a single day’: This figure is a rough estimate by financial media outlets and overseas news sources (such as Intellectia and NBC commentary) based on post-market fluctuations in market capitalisation, the main text of this report is based on the official closing data showing that Alphabet lost approximately US$500 billion in market capitalisation in a single day (varying slightly across sources to around US$480 billion based on closing prices) and that the Nasdaq Composite Index closed down 2.86 per cent on 30 October;
  3. Regarding Alphabet’s earnings per share (EPS) metrics: The figure of US$2.12 disclosed includes unrealised gains or losses on changes in the fair value of equity investments recognised in accordance with accounting standards; CNBC’s adjusted operating figure is approximately US$2.67, and this should be viewed objectively in the context of non-cash distortions when analysing the figures;
  4. The Commercial Nature of Microsoft’s 10 Billion Revenue: The US$10 billion in revenue from the OpenAI arrangement disclosed in the financial statements essentially comprises offsets for Azure cloud resources and a revenue-sharing agreement; this constitutes a special model involving a deep strategic partnership and cannot be directly equated with standard enterprise-grade SaaS revenue;
  5. A Comparison of Timelines from the 1990s: The application of a time-reversed simulation—comparing the historical telecommunications bubble with current AI investment—forms part of the author’s macro-historical analytical framework, which aims to reveal the tension between macro-statistical lags and micro-level leading indicators; it does not constitute an absolute, mechanical repetition of history.

Conclusion: Computing power is strong, but cash flow is even stronger

Looking back on this dramatic week, the clash between macro and micro factors has, in fact, served as a timely wake-up call.

What macroeconomists see is the most spectacular wave of machinery installation in human history—hundreds of terawatts of transformers being hoisted into place, tens of thousands of state-of-the-art silicon wafers emitting a low rumble within server rooms, with 3.4 per cent of GDP faithfully recording every instance of capital extravagance that takes place on this land.

But from the sidelines of the stock market, investors finally posed that most basic—and most devastating—question of business common sense:

If annual capital expenditure of US$220 billion results in a net outflow of free cash flow exceeding US$40 billion over the past 12 months (Amazon TTM), how much longer can this frenzy possibly last?

The latest development this week is that Amazon has disclosed a supplier financing arrangement of up to US$80 billion in its quarterly filings, using the hardware in its data centres as collateral for the debt—Where cash flow ends, the debt market begins。

There is no doubt about the demand for AI, no doubt about the explosive growth of cloud services, and certainly no doubt about the long-term vision of technology transforming the world. But as the ironclad rule of the capital markets states:

Computing power may be robust, but free cash flow is always more robust than computing power.

Whilst the tech giants raise their glasses at the macro-level banquet to celebrate growth exceeding 3.4 per cent, the micro-level reckoning has already begun in cold-blooded fashion. Only those who can be the first to truly transform red-hot computing power into cold, abundant free cash flow will survive the ebb of the next capital cycle.
