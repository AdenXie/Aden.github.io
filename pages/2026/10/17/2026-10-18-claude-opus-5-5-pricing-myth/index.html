The price reduction myth of Claude Opus 5.5: Engineering progress or a price war before the IPO?

AdenXie
  2026-10-17 17:32:46 2026-10-17 17:32:46 Created  
  • AI and Technology 
 
  • large model 
  • | Claude 
  • | Anthropic 
  • | Codex

I initially liked Opus 5.5. The model is stronger, and the price has dropped again, which is certainly something to be happy about. I subscribe to both Claude Pro and ChatGPT Plus, and I also actually use Claude Code and Codex, so I have reason to expect that competition will ultimately bring benefits to users.

But after reading my own consumption records, AA tests, and putting Luo Fuli's post about reasoning cost reduction together with Anthropic's expansion and fault records, I am increasingly reluctant to accept the story of "stronger and cheaper" at face value.

I now tend to see it more as a price competition before the IPO window: competing for users and demonstrating growth by lowering prices, while still having to deal with the issues of expensive computing power supply and service delivery. This is my judgment, not that I have access to Anthropic's internal decision-making documents. What I want to ask is, how much of the engineering costs behind the price reduction have been explained, and how much of the growth that investors like will be realized as benefits for users.

Who exactly is the "40% discount" compared to?

In its release notes dated September 22, 2026, Anthropic described Opus 5.5 as a new model with improved capabilities and reduced operating costs. Its claim of a "40% reduction in typical workload" is compared to... Opus 5The condition is the default setting in its testing; This does not mean it is 40% cheaper than all competing models.Publish the original article

Official documentation confirms that the typical input price per million tokens has decreased from $5 to $4, and the output price has decreased from $25 to $20, representing a 20% decrease. Cache reads dropped from $0.50 to $0.20, a 60% decrease. Opus 5.5 costs $5 for a five-minute cache write and $8 for an hour write.Pricing and Migration Documentation

Therefore, 40% is not a flat discount that can be read directly from a price list. It mixes price reductions for different Token categories and consumption changes under specific tasks. This percentage may change by changing the cache ratio, the inference level, or the task.

The official text lists the control subjects and test conditions; What I criticize is the appeal of this product narrative: the previous generation is the price benchmark, the advantage task is the capability benchmark, and the combination of the two makes "stronger and cheaper" an easily acceptable reason to buy. But it still hasn't answered for me whether the Opus 5.5 is worth the price given today's other options.

In my statistics, the most easily overlooked aspect is caching.

I bought Claude Pro and ChatGPT Plus. The following CC Switch amounts are API equivalent fees, used to observe the consumption structure, and are not additional bills.

In the last 30 days, claude-opus-5-5 under Claude Code has received 1264 requests, totaling approximately 360M tokens, with an equivalent fee of $118.58. Codex mixes multiple GPT models, has 4,612 requests, approximately 623M tokens, and an equivalent fee of $168.75. Previously, focusing on the total volume, I would have thought that Codex was used much more. Divide by the number of requests, and the screen changes again.

I also need to explain my daily settings: Opus 5.5 mainly uses Medium, occasionally High; GPT-6.1 Sol mainly uses High, and sometimes Medium. The total number of Codex also includes other GPT models, and the entire statistical table cannot be considered a representation of a single GPT-6.1 Sol model. The same inference level from different manufacturers does not represent the same calculation budget.

Claude averages about $0.0938 per request, while Codex averages about $0.0366, with the former being about 2.56 times the latter. The average total tokens are approximately 284,800 and 135,100 respectively, representing a ratio of approximately 2.11 times.

But a request is not a task. A single tool call may generate requests, and a job may be broken down into many requests; Claude Code and Codex have different tooling and context management, and the latter also mixes multiple models. This set of numbers describes my two usage combinations and cannot be rewritten as "Claude is 2.56 times more expensive to complete the same task".

This set of personal records does not have uniform tasks, acceptance results, and rework times, but it clearly shows how my usage portfolio consumes resources. It gave me reason to keep asking. Consumers also have the right to understand products based on their own work, rather than forever remaining stuck in the success stories selected by manufacturers.

With a hit rate of 98.8% in the cache, why are there still so many fees?

Claude's statistics show that the cache hit rate is 98.8%, with cache reads of approximately 355MB, writes of approximately 4.46MB, and outputs of approximately 1.27MB. The newly added normal input only displays 2682 tokens; the cause of the anomaly has not yet been confirmed.

Based on verified prices, and with writes uniformly estimated in five-minute increments: cache reads are approximately $71, outputs are $25.40, cache writes are $22.30, and regular inputs are approximately $0.01, for a total of approximately $118.71. This is approximately $0.13 different from the $118.58 shown in the software. Rounding the summary value may cause differences, and details such as write duration also need to be confirmed by the log.

355M cache reads account for approximately 98.6% of the total tokens displayed, but still account for approximately 59.8% of the recalculation fee. The previous percentage is the number I obtained by dividing by the summary, and should not be confused with the 98.8% hit rate displayed by the software.

Cache hits mean reusing content that has already been processed, usually eliminating some of the overhead of repeated processing. It has a discount, and it still charges. The cache unit price is very low, and when multiplied by a sufficiently large amount of reuse, it can become a major source of cost.

Fixing the usage for this set and recalculating it at the old Opus 5 rate, the cost is approximately $237.14; At the Opus 5.5 rate, the price is approximately $118.71, which is almost half. The price reduction for cache access alone saved approximately $106.50, accounting for about 90% of the total savings in this rate recalculation.

This figure shows that for a combination like mine, which has a very high proportion of cache, the price reduction is indeed significant. It also clarifies the structure of the "price reduction": most of the savings come from discounts that repeatedly read the context. The Token usage is fixed here, and the change in consumption of the two generations of models to complete the same task is not measured; My records can therefore neither verify nor disprove the official 40% claim of typical load. The extent to which the price reduction has been implemented, and how expensive it is compared to competitors, are questions that will be answered separately next.

The differences I see between the two toolsets are also concentrated here: Claude reads an average of about 280,900 cache tokens per request, while Codex reads about 130,100. Outputs approximately 1005 and 449 tokens, respectively. The context carried repeatedly is much larger than the final output. If we only focus on "how many words are written in the answer" when discussing Agent fees, we will miss a lot of historical content that is read repeatedly.

A high cache hit rate does not individually justify the efficiency of the entire workflow. What history must be preserved, what calls are worth making, and how much acceptable results are ultimately produced determine whether these expenses are worth spending.

For subscribers, there is no publicly available, fixed conversion table between the API equivalent fee and the package amount that I can recalculate item by item. You can't use this $118.58 to directly predict how quickly I'll peak. What I want to know is whether the same subscription can stably complete more work, and whether I need to interrupt the waiting period or add more credit. This record does not cover these results, so it provides tracing clues and is not yet a subscription cost-effectiveness experiment.

AA's lead also comes with a cost tag.

The Artificial Analysis page offers another observation. As of October 18, the "max with fallback" configuration of Opus 5.5 had an IQ of 58 and a cost of $5.98 per IQ task. The GPT-6 Astra has a max configuration of $53 and $3.26. The former scores higher and costs about 1.83 times more than the latter.Opus Page、Astra Page

Between these two configurations, Opus scores higher and costs more to test. Whether the capability gain is worth the price difference depends on what problem it solves. An index of 58 to 53 does not mean that productivity increased by about 9%, and dividing it by the score does not give me the benefit of my work.

AA tested the Max configuration, which is different from my daily gear; The Opus line also includes a fallback. These conditions should be seen along with the numbers.

The AA method page explains that Smart Index prioritizes using the number of tokens reported by the vendor API to calculate fees; This is different from the way speed testing uses a unified tokenizer. Cost is also measured using benchmark weights and caching, and the "max" in all tags cannot be treated as the same computational budget.AA Method Description

AA's output Token diagram illustrates this cost more intuitively: Opus 5.5 Max with fallback has a weighted average total output of approximately 119K per task, of which approximately 84K is inference; The total output of GPT-6 Astra Max is labeled as approximately 27K, and GPT-6 Sol Max is labeled as approximately 38K. 119K already contains the inference section; we cannot add another 84K.

Based on the total amount shown in the screenshot, Opus is approximately 4.4 times that of Astra and 3.1 times that of Sol. There are slight discrepancies between the sub-items in the screenshot and the total number, which may involve individual rounding. It is not advisable to infer the exact original number based on these displayed values. The screenshot also does not show the acquisition time and index version, making it inappropriate to combine it with the real-time page to form an accurate historical experimental record.

These multiples measure the billing Token consumption shown in the graph, not the computing power or waste. However, when a lower unit price is considered an advantage, more consumption should also be discussed. What results did the extra tokens yield that were essential for achieving them? If this part of the revenue doesn't go into my task, my leading position on the list won't offset my usage costs.

We must also confront a set of materials that differ from the direction of the above results: Anthropic, in the same release page, claims that the default Medium level of Opus 5.5 is on FrontierCode, exceeding the highest score of GPT-6 Astra by about one-fifth of the task cost. This is not the same test as AA's Max composite index: the benchmarks, inference gears, and workflows are different. It illustrates that cost advantages can reverse with tasks and settings, and that extracting just one favorable chart can tell a very different product story. What I want to see is which side my typical work falls on.

Luo Fuli's words made me even more eager to ask about Anthropic's engineering accounts.

Luo Fuli's post about MiMo API price reductions is a piece of material I didn't want to miss. The following screenshot of the Chinese translation of X matches the corresponding English archive content: She placed the largest price reduction, up to 99%, on the cache hit input, saying that the hierarchical KV cache optimization of the inference framework increased the cache capacity by about five times, which is equivalent to a reduction in cache cost of about 80%. She also mentioned the original profit margin of about 2–3 times, and the judgment that the new price would basically break even when operating at near full capacity.English Archive、Original post address

The value of this passage lies in putting price reduction back into the relationship between engineering and business: how can caching become cheaper, how much room was left in the original pricing, and why are they willing to give it up now? These figures are still the team leader's own statements and are not audit conclusions, but at least they provide a mechanism for questioning.

Xiaomi's technical article further explains that Hybrid SWA can reduce KVCache storage to about 1/7 of the full-attention structure; Cache management, prefix matching, and scheduling need to be changed together to realize architectural benefits. DeepSeek's earlier V3/R1 system description also openly discusses the separation of prefilling and decoding, expert parallelism, and load balancing. Local revenue does not equal the cost of the entire service, but the discussion has a specific target.Xiaomi Technology Original、DeepSeek System Description

Looking back at Anthropic, the 60% reduction in cache read quotes is not evidence that KVCache's resource costs have also decreased by 60%. The Opus 5.5 release and migration notes I found concluded that the service compute requirements would be lower, but they did not provide sufficient pre- and post-project accounts to allow me to review the changes in cache unit costs. Without this account, it cannot be concluded that it is completely unoptimized; Similarly, the price reduction cannot be directly used as proof of a breakthrough in the project.

Therefore, I brought in Luo Fuli not to advertise for another company. I want to ask Anthropic: How much of this price reduction comes from already achieved project cost reductions, and how much from the redistribution of profit margins and the need to seize market share? The latter is also a normal business practice, but consumers do not need to automatically regard it as a technological dividend, let alone place their trust in a new price list.

Computing power can be purchased from Musk; stable delivery cannot be replaced by press conferences.

The so-called "borrowing computing power from Musk" is more accurately described as a cooperation in computing power procurement. On May 6, Anthropic announced that it would use the full computing capacity of SpaceX Colossus 1, stating that it would gain more than 300 megawatts of additional capacity with more than 220,000 NVIDIA GPUs, and linked the expansion directly to the increase in Claude user quotas. The partner also issued a corresponding announcement.Anthropic Announcements、Partner Announcement

There's nothing shameful about purchasing external computing power. However, this shows that the amount of capacity a service can provide to users is still closely related to external supply. Buying computing resources and using each unit of resources more efficiently are two different things. Expansion announcements cannot replace proof of efficiency.

My assessment of Anthropic's service engineering capabilities is not as high as my assessment of its model capabilities. According to the UTC date on the official status page, there was an increase in error rates on September 29th that simultaneously affected the webpage, Claude Code, Cowork, and API. On October 6th, Opus 5.5 experienced another increase in error rates; On October 7, some organizations were incorrectly judged to have reached their spending limit, resulting in requests for products such as APIs being rejected. These are all delivery issues that require users to bear.September 29 Record、October 6 Record、October 7 Record

This official status page snapshot also retains exception records for multiple products, with the API annotating an availability rate of 99.59% over the past 90 days. It is not a horizontal ranking of the entire industry, nor does it prove that every failure is caused by a lack of computing power; Console report failures should not always be considered inference API outages. But these delivery records are enough to make me refuse to give a high score to the engineering capabilities of the entire company based solely on model scores.Official status page

The ability of a model to solve problems during testing and the ability of a paid service to consistently meet demand are two different capabilities. For a company, the former is worth promoting, and the latter should also accept criticism. Cheaper tokens cannot make up for the time lost when a job is disrupted by errors and interruptions.

I prefer to understand the price reductions before the IPO as a price war.

This is also the business background that makes me wary of "stronger and cheaper". According to a Reuters report based on its prospectus, Anthropic will have revenue of nearly $4.6 billion in 2025, an operating loss of more than $8 billion, and will undertake a huge commitment to computing power investment. The report also discussed the launch of Opus 5.5 ahead of competition against GPT-6 Astra and an expected IPO.Reuters reports

In October, Reuters, citing investor event participants, reported that the company's third-quarter revenue reached $16.8 billion and told investors that it was profitable on an adjusted basis for the quarter. Last year's losses, adjusted profits, and future investments should be read separately.Reported on October 14

Given this window of opportunity, I prefer to interpret price reductions as a business move to compete for market share and maintain growth expectations. Lower prices have the opportunity to bring in more calls and customers, and make the story of "strong demand, scale expansion, and improved efficiency" easier to tell to investors. It doesn't need to be proven to be a comprehensive underlying engineering breakthrough first, and there are good commercial reasons for it to happen.

This is exactly what I want to criticize: why do users automatically interpret price competition as a technological victory for businesses, and then treat unrealized economies of scale as their own benefits in advance? I have no evidence to suggest that Anthropic is dumping at a loss or intentionally deceiving, nor do I have evidence to prove that it will definitely cut its quota in the future; But I can perfectly refuse to endorse its services and prices with such a growth narrative.

The unit price of Opus 5.5 has indeed dropped, and the amount I use is also cheaper at the new rate. The problem is that the reference for the price reduction is its own old price; Faced with competitors' Token consumption, user task costs, and real delivery stability, the calculations are far from over.

I still acknowledge Claude's modeling abilities and am still willing to pay for a truly useful product. But what I want to say about this price reduction is: don't rush to interpret it as a win for users. The company needs a price competition, but also a compelling IPO story; What I need is a tool that can get the job done consistently. There is no reason for the interests of the two to be tacitly accepted as the same thing.
