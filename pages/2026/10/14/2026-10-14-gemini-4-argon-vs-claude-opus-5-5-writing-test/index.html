Origin and test environment

The conclusion is first: after four rounds of testing, both models have their strengths and weaknesses in "speaking human language". Claude Opus 5.5 is better in terms of sense of proportion and "non-fabrication" of SMS messages; Longer articles in Gemini 4 Argon are smoother, but the default writing style is heavy. Neither of them escaped the AI-driven tone when it came to writing long articles.

In my daily use, I've noticed that recent versions of GPT and Gemini are somewhat "non-human". Coincidentally, Gemini 4 Argon was released on October 14, 2026, and I heard that Claude Opus 5.5 had put some effort into writing it, so I tested the two together on the same day.

Test Environment:

  • Gemini 4 Argon: Gemini web version, mode selection "Argon High Point", no developer tools used.
  • Claude Opus 5.5: Used in Claude Code, with a thinking intensity of "medium".
  • GPT did not participate in this test.
  • Each problem and each model was run only once.

Question design and item-by-item scoring were done in another Claude Opus 5.5 dialogue. In other words,Claude is rating himself.This is the biggest source of deviation in this article, and readers can make their own judgments by referring to the original text below. The model output cited in this article is the original text, with only information that can point to individuals replaced with code names, such as "Person A", "School X", "Major X", and "City X". The rest of the content remains unchanged.

Test methods and scoring checklist

The test consists of four rounds, each increasing the difficulty of the problems exposed in the previous round:

  1. Default writing styleOnly give the topic, and do not write "speak in plain language" or "use plain language" in the prompts, otherwise it will become a test of whether it will obey instructions.
  2. Go to AI cavityI explicitly request that memorable quotes, metaphors, and sublimations be removed, and that I not fabricate my experiences.
  3. Ask a question firstTell the model it doesn't know my experience and allow it to ask me first.
  4. Insufficient materialsGive me a very thin piece of material and see if it will be embellished.

Round 1, Topic 1: Explaining "overfitting" to parents

This question is tied on both sides, both scoring 8 points, and most of the questions are in plain language.

Hint words:

My parents didn't know anything about computers, and they asked me what "overfitting" meant in the machine learning I was learning. Help me write an explanation for them.

Gemini 4 Argon Original text

Mom and Dad, you can think of training computers (machine learning) as teaching a student to prepare for an exam, while "overfitting" means that the student has memorized the questions and won't be able to apply them to other situations.

When we teach computers to recognize objects, it's like giving students a workbook. Ideally, after completing several sets of tests, students should summarize the problem-solving patterns so that they can do the same when they encounter new problems.

But when "overfitting" occurs, the computer is too rigid—it doesn't understand the underlying patterns, but instead memorizes every question in the workbook, even the typos and question numbers. And the result is:

  • Regular test (using old data): 100 points because I memorized all the questions.
  • Real exam (encountering new situations): It's a complete mess; you just need to change the numbers on the question and you won't know anything at all.

To give another real-life example: Let's say I only use our "brown teddy dog" to teach the computer to recognize dogs. If the computer "overfits," it will rigidly believe that "only dogs are brown, curly-haired, small, or even have a red bell around their necks." When a large golden retriever is brought to it tomorrow, it won't recognize it.

Therefore, a large part of my work is to prevent computers from "rote memorization," to teach them to grasp key points, find patterns, and truly apply what they have learned to other situations.

Claude Opus 5.5 Original text

The version that can be sent directly to parents:

What is "overfitting"?

For example, it's like a child preparing for an exam.

There was a student whose teacher gave him a workbook. He memorized the answers to every question in it by heart. He was tested on the questions in the workbook and got perfect marks every time. But when it comes to the official exam, if the question is slightly different in number or wording, he won't know how. Because he only memorized the answers and didn't truly understand the principles behind solving the problems.

The same applies to computer "learning". We show the computer many examples and let it summarize the patterns on its own. For example, show it thousands of photos of cats to help it learn to recognize cats. If it "overlearns," it will write down the details in these photos as patterns, such as "cats are all on the sofa" or "cats have white walls in the background." As a result, we tested it with photos it had seen, and they were all correct; If you take a new photo, such as a cat on the grass, it won't recognize it.

This situation where someone performs well on practice questions but fails when faced with new problems is called "overfitting".

One of the important tasks of our research is to make computers learn real patterns like good students, rather than rote memorization, so that they can make correct judgments even when they encounter new situations they have never seen before.

If you want it to be shorter, you can just keep the first paragraph of the exam metaphor and the last sentence of the summary. Parents usually understand it immediately.

Round 1, Topic 2: Real Feelings of the First Semester of Studying Abroad

This question is worth 5 points on both sides, but the problems are different: Gemini is a fancy motivational quote, while Claude is a plain template that fabricates experiences for the user.

Hint words:

I'm writing an article for my juniors who are preparing to pursue graduate studies abroad, sharing their most authentic feelings about the first semester of graduate school.

It should be noted that Claude's conversations have a memory function enabled, so it knows the user's school and major background, while Gemini does not. This allowed Claude to write more specifically, which directly led to the fiction problem mentioned below.

Gemini 4 Argon Original text

To you about to embark on a long journey: The first semester of graduate school, those real moments no one told you in advance

The moment you receive your acceptance letter, apply for your visa, and fill your suitcase, you probably think that the script for pursuing a master's degree abroad is an academic journey full of exotic flavor. But when you actually land, dragging two large suitcases weighing nearly 30 kilograms in front of an empty mattress in an unfamiliar apartment, the true face of the first semester of graduate school is just beginning to be revealed.

"Hard landing" without buffer period

Many people think that the first two weeks abroad are a "honeymoon period" for adjusting to the time difference, shopping at IKEA, and taking photos to post on social media. In fact, the pace of graduate school is so fast that it doesn't give you any breathing room. You might be struggling on Monday to get a bank card, connect to Wi-Fi, or distinguish between light and dark soy sauce from all-English labels at the supermarket, but on Tuesday you were already sitting in the lecture hall facing dozens of pages of Reading material handed out by the professor and the first week's pre-class quiz. Life's trivialities and academic pressures don't come one after the other; they hit you simultaneously before you've even figured out your way home.

The academic gap from "being fed" to "finding one's own way"

During undergraduate studies, teachers often break down the knowledge points from the textbook and explain them to you. But in the graduate classroom, the professor assumes that you have mastered the basic tools. In a two-hour class, the PowerPoint presentations were flipped through like a revolving lantern. The class only discussed the core framework and cutting-edge controversies. The rest of the derivation, code implementation, or literature review was all done by myself in the library after class.

A more real challenge comes from high-intensity output in an all-English environment. In the first few weeks, you might have looked up a screen full of new words the night before and read the literature thoroughly. The next day, when you go to a Seminar, you still need to race your brain for a few seconds before you can get through to a classmate who speaks at such a fast pace. When it comes to Group projects, you also have to learn how to deal with team members from different cultural backgrounds and routines while advancing the progress. Almost everyone has experienced that frustrating feeling of "trying so hard, but still feeling like you know nothing" late at night in October.

Folded Time: A Three-Front Battle Between Study, Life, and Job Search

If a four-year undergraduate degree is a marathon, then a one- to two-year master's degree is more like a 100-meter sprint. What causes the most anxiety is often not the coursework itself, but rather...Everything is crammed into the same timeline.。

Many students have only been in school for two weeks and haven't even familiarized themselves with the campus map before having to join the ranks of those seeking jobs in the fall recruitment or local areas. During the day, I attend classes and revise group reports. In the evening, I go to the supermarket to buy discounted meat and vegetables to cook for myself. After washing the dishes and sitting back at my desk at 9 p.m., I also have to revise my resume, do practice questions, and prepare for video interviews across time zones. You will suddenly realize that your parents used to shield you from so many fragments of life; When cooking, washing clothes, fixing light bulbs, and calculating exchange rates all become your own business, even just getting a hot meal on time and seven hours of sleep can be considered a remarkable form of self-discipline.

From "social anxiety" to learning to reconcile with loneliness

During the first few days of Orientation, you might attend various gatherings and suddenly have dozens of new friends on your phone. But as soon as classes officially start, everyone will quickly return to their respective lives. Graduate students' social interactions are very direct and realistic. There are no fixed classes, class schedules vary, and everyone is in a hurry.

Gradually, you will go through a period of extreme quiet: going to the library alone to reserve a seat, going to the supermarket alone with headphones on, and walking home alone with coffee in the evening. You may feel lonely at first, but perhaps after the midterm exams, you will suddenly fall in love with this undisturbed freedom.Transforming "loneliness" into "solitude" is the most important compulsory course in the first semester of studying abroad. When you stop forcing yourself to socialize in order to fit in, and focus your energy on your sleep, meals, and books, your mind will actually calm down.

A final piece of truth

If you doubt yourself late at night during your first semester because of incomprehensible literature, incompatible data, or a burnt dinner, please remember:This panic is absolutely normal, not because you are not good enough, but because you are simultaneously adapting to a new language, a new set of academic rules, and a completely independent life.

In the first semester, don't strive for perfection in everything. Allow yourself to get distracted occasionally in class, allow your first draft to be written in a stumbling manner, and follow the principle of "finish first, then perfect" to deal with the mountain of tasks. When you feel like you can't take it anymore, don't force yourself. Make a video call home, or simply close your computer and go to sleep.

After surviving these most chaotic first three months, when you finish submitting your last final paper and walk out of the library into the evening breeze of a foreign street, you will be surprised to find that the version of yourself who used to have to look up translation software even when buying groceries has already grown a tough armor without you even realizing it. Wishing you a smooth start and a first semester of your own.

Claude Opus 5.5 Original text

My most authentic feelings during the first semester of graduate school

Oct 14, 2026 · @PeopleA

Written in front

To summarize the first semester in one sentence: It was more tiring than I imagined, but also more worthwhile than I imagined.

I studied major Y in China for my undergraduate degree, and came to school X in a certain month this year to study major X. Before setting off, I read a lot of travel guides, and I checked everything clearly, including course selection, renting a place, and membership card application. But what really caught me off guard were things that were rarely mentioned in those travel guides. This article doesn't discuss the process, but only the feelings. I hope you have a better understanding before you set off.

Pace: So fast that there's no time to adapt

School X has a three-semester system, with only about 10 weeks of teaching time per semester. In China, a course can be learned slowly over a semester. Here, homework begins in the second week, and there are quizzes or project milestones in the fourth or fifth week.

My biggest feeling is:There is no "adaptation period"。 Having just finished adjusting to the time difference and found the classroom, the first deadline is already right in front of us. By the time you feel like you've "finally figured it out," final exams have arrived.

So don't expect to gradually get into the swing of things after school starts. For classes that can be previewed in advance, review the first week's content before you set off.

Language: Passing the test doesn't mean you can understand it.

IELTS scores are just the ticket. When actually teaching, the teacher speaks fast, with different accents, and uses a lot of professional vocabulary. I was already lucky if I could understand half of the lectures in the first week; I relied entirely on watching the recording after class.

What's even more difficult than listening to lectures isopen mouth。 During the tutorial, everyone was rushing to speak. I had organized a sentence in my mind, and the topic had already passed. During group discussions, people clearly had ideas, but remained silent for fear of saying the wrong thing.

Later I discovered that my classmates actually rarely pay attention to grammar; what they care about is whether you contribute your viewpoints. It's okay if you don't say it perfectly, but not saying it will really be a loss.

Learning method: No one is pushing you

Undergraduate programs in China have classes, counselors, fixed class schedules, and classmates, and someone will remind you of many things. Here, no one takes attendance, no one presses for homework, and all course information is available on websites and forums. Whether you read it or not is entirely up to you.

The homework is also different. Many programming assignments only give a vague requirement, and you have to decide the rest of the design, testing, and report writing yourself. Group projects are more challenging: division of labor, communication, and schedule coordination take more time than the code itself.

There is another point that must be emphasized:Academic integrity。 References should be standardized, code should not be copied arbitrarily, and discussions with classmates should also be balanced. This matter is taken seriously here.

Life: Loneliness is the norm, not failure

In the first few weeks after arriving, novelty overwhelms everything. Beach, coffee, blue sky—I want to take pictures and post them on my WeChat Moments every day.

After the novelty wears off, loneliness will slowly emerge. One person cooks, one person meets deadlines, and when they get sick, they have to go to the pharmacy by themselves. It's nighttime in China, but early morning here, so you have to take into account the time difference to talk to someone.

I want to tell you: this feeling is normal, almost everyone has experienced it. It doesn't mean you're not suited to studying abroad; it just means you're experiencing true independence.

A few suggestions for you

  1. Don't choose a semester that is too full. Give yourself room to adapt first, and your grades and performance will be more stable.
  2. List the deadlines for all classes in the first week of school. Stick it on the wall or put it in a calendar, and 10 weeks will go by faster than you think.
  3. Recording is a lifeline, but don't rely on it. If possible, go to the site. If you have any questions, ask the teacher or tutor on the spot.
  4. Take the initiative to speak, even if it stumbles. Saying one sentence in a tutorial is more useful than memorizing ten words after class.
  5. Make good use of school resources. Academic tutoring, writing centers, psychological counseling, and employment services are all free, and many people don't use them until graduation.
  6. Make a few friends who can look out for each other. It doesn't have to be a lot, but someone who can lend a hand when you're sick or have a breakdown.
  7. Thinking about job hunting as soon as possible. The timeline for internships and campus recruitment starts earlier than you might think, and you can start learning about it in the first semester.

in the end

Looking back on the first semester, the hardest weeks were precisely the times when I grew the fastest. I learned to manage my own time, express my opinions in English, and live a good life on my own.

If you are preparing to go abroad right now, you don't need to think about studying abroad too much, nor do you need to think about it too difficult. It is an experience that requires you to give it your all and will definitely make you stronger.

Wishing you all the best, see you in City X.
