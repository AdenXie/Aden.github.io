Serbia’s Economic Crossroads Amid Political Upheaval: Lithium Mines, Russian Gas and ‘Ironclad Friendship’

Introduction: In our post last week on‘The Synchrony of the Wave of Protests in Europe’In our in-depth analysis, we examined how high interest rates and energy inflation had sparked protests amongst young people in Southern Europe and France. Just a few days later, this wave of youth protests sweeping across Europe reached the Balkans, where it entered its ‘second phase, focusing on governance issues’: On 25 October 2026, Serbia’s snap parliamentary election produced a historic upset: the ‘Students’ List’, a party with no prior governing experience, secured an absolute majority, bringing an end to the Vucic camp’s 14-year rule. However, once the euphoria of the election had subsided, what lay before this young team were not abstract political slogans, but three weighty volumes of transnational economic figures, each with far-reaching implications:Will Rio Tinto’s lithium mine in Europe—valued at nearly US$3 billion—be kept open or shut down? How can a smooth transition be achieved between the cheap Russian gas, the supply of which is renewed every three months, and the state-owned refinery that has just changed hands? And what will become of the China-Serbia infrastructure investment project, which provides vital support for the nation’s industrial lifeline and tens of thousands of jobs?

Introduction: The counter-intuitive ‘vote of confidence’ cast by bond traders

Late on the night of 25 October 2026, the atmosphere in the vote-counting hall in Belgrade was electric.

Just two hours after the polls closed, Vucic—the strongman who had dominated Serbian politics for 14 years (and who had previously resigned as president to stand for prime minister)—took to the podium ahead of schedule to concede defeat. Formed by university students and young academics,“Student List – Students Win”The Alliance secured approximately 2.2 million votes, winning seats in the 250-seat parliament 135 to 140 seats (exit polls put the figure at 138 seats), surpassing the threshold of 126 seats to achieveA majority on its own; whilst the ruling coalition secured only around 105 seats. The turnout across the country was close to 69 per cent, reaching a historic high not seen for decades.

In a subsequent column, Bloomberg characterised this dramatic upheaval as ‘a group of faceless young people taking control of the state’.

However, on the first trading day following the announcement of the election results, the reaction of the capital markets took everyone by surprise:

The first to make their position clear were not Brussels, nor Moscow, but international sovereign bond traders.

Figure 1Post-election market movements in Serbia: yields on US dollar-denominated bonds maturing in 2036 fell to 6.42 per cent (a one-month low), whilst 10-year euro-denominated bonds fell by 17 basis points; S&P maintained its BBB- investment-grade rating, reflecting an initial positive response from the capital markets to the easing of political risks.

On 27 October, yields on Serbia’s benchmark US dollar-denominated sovereign bonds maturing in 2036 fell rather than rose, dropping by 12–13 basis points to 6.42%, hitting a new low in nearly a month; the yield on 10-year offshore euro-denominated sovereign bonds also fell sharply by 17 basis points to 5.19%. Rather than witnessing a panic-driven flight of capital, the market responded by investing real money in sovereign bonds, thereby casting its first vote of confidence in this young team with absolutely no track record in government.

However, in stark contrast to the buoyancy in the bond market, Belgrade’s local share index, the Belex15, rose only slightly throughout the day +0.23%; Strickland Metals, a mining company listed in Australia and with gold exploration projects in Serbia, saw its share price brieflyA sharp fall, with the share price dropping by as much as 33 per cent during the session。

Behind the cautious approach to ‘debt-driven’ stocks lies an extremely complex dual mindset in the capital markets：

International creditors welcomed the peaceful resolution of the long-standing political deadlock and public opposition; however, in a commentary issued on 28 October, the international credit rating agency Fitch sounded a warning—Although electoral uncertainty has diminished, the new administration’s economic agenda, policy priorities and cabinet formation remain a mystery.。

The 90-day countdown to forming a government, as stipulated by the Constitution, is about to begin. The young elected representatives will soon discover that in this landlocked nation situated on the geopolitical fault line between East and West,Taking a political stance is an extremely cheap exercise, whilst the hard realities of capital and energy leave no room for black-and-white thinking.。

I. The Life and Death of Europe’s Largest Lithium Mine: The 20-Year Struggle at Jadar

If there is one asset capable of simultaneously influencing Brussels’ green ambitions, the balance sheets of international mining giants and street politics in Serbia, it must surely be the one located in the west of the country:The Jadar lithium-boron deposit。

Figure 2A 20-year timeline for the Jadar lithium mine: Rio Tinto’s investment commitment was increased from $2.4B to $2.95B (put on hold in November 2025); the EU designated it a strategic project under the CRMA; and, following a change in government after the general election, the project faces a licence expiry deadline in 2027.

1. Europe’s ‘white gold’, dormant for 20 years

In 2004, geologists working for the global mining giant Rio Tinto discovered a unique lithium-bearing borate mineral in the Jadar Valley in Serbia, which was subsequently officially named ‘Jadarite’.

Following nearly twenty years of exploration and feasibility studies, this mineral deposit has been confirmed asThe largest and highest-grade undeveloped lithium deposit on the European continent：

  • Resource reserves: Proven ore reserves amount to 118 million tonnes, with an average lithium oxide (Li₂O) grade of up to 1.8%；
  • Design capacity: Estimated annual output once full production is achieved 58,000 metric tonnes of battery-grade lithium carbonate, 160,000 metric tonnes of boric acid and 255,000 metric tonnes of sodium sulphate;
  • Industry standing: According to estimates by the industry think tank AlphaBriefing, the mine’s production capacity alone is sufficient to meet10 per cent to 15 per cent of total demand for electric vehicle batteries in Europe... Over its entire life cycle (40 years), it is expected to produce 2.3 million metric tonnes of lithium carbonate, which would be sufficient to propel Serbia directly into the ranks of the world’s top ten lithium-producing countries.

Figure 3Rio Tinto’s Yadal lithium mine accounts: cumulative investment of approximately 640 million euros (€640M), the project’s total budget has been reassessed at US$2.95 billion ($2.95B), with the licence due to expire in 2027; an annual output of 58,000 metric tonnes of lithium carbonate is sufficient to reshape Europe’s electric vehicle supply chain.

2. Abolition, Revival and Tying to Transnational Strategies

The political tug-of-war surrounding this mega-mine has been almost the most intense domestic political storm to hit Serbia over the past five years:

  • July 2021: Rio Tinto has formally approved up to US$2.4 billion ($2.4B) commitments to investment in the construction of;
  • 20 January 2022: Following months of roadblock protests in more than 40 towns and cities across the country, the Burnačić government, under pressure from the general election, announced the revocation of the spatial planning order for the project (the Constitutional Court subsequently ruled that this revocation order was unconstitutional);
  • July 2024: The Vučić government issued a new decree in accordance with a ruling by the Constitutional Court, reviving the project at short notice, prompting tens of thousands of people in the capital to take to the streets for a mass environmental rally;
  • 4 June 2025: The European Commission, keen to reduce its reliance on key raw materials from overseas, has officially included Jadar among the first 13 entities outside the EUStrategic Projects under the Critical Raw Materials Act (CRMA), linking it directly to the security of Europe’s electric vehicle strategy.

3. Rio Tinto’s retreat and the sword of Damocles hanging over 2027

However, on 13 November 2025, Bloomberg reported that Rio Tinto had officially placed the project into ‘care and maintenance’ status, with the total budget for the project being reassessed and adjusted to US$2.95 billion ($2.95B)。

It must be pointed out that,Rio Tinto’s decision to put the project on hold at the time was not entirely due to local political protests：

A more crucial commercial factor is that, against the backdrop of a global slump in lithium prices, Rio Tinto’s new management has implemented strict capital discipline and cost-cutting measures, with Jadar becoming one of the key projects targeted for active downsizing. By the end of 2025, Rio Tinto will have invested Approximately 640 million euros (€640M) the actual sunk costs.

Rio Tinto’s current statutory exploration and mining licences are due to expire on Due to expire in 2027. There is now just over a year left for the various parties to engage in mediation.

4. The new government’s dilemma: anti-mining allies versus the EU’s strategic carrot

For the newly elected ‘student list’, the Yadar lithium mine is a deeply divisive political issue that cannot be avoided:

  • The Shackles of Public Opinion: According to reports from several Balkan media outlets, Zlatko Kokanović, the most prominent local environmental activist and leader of the anti-mining movement in the Jadral Valley, is himself a key figure within the ‘Students’ List’ coalition. Should the new government give the green light to the project, it would amount to a direct betrayal of its grassroots support base;
  • Pressure from the EU: Whilst congratulating the country on the success of the election, the EU has made no secret of its expectations regarding the CRMA strategic project. The conditions offered by Brussels are extremely attractive—integrating Jadar into the European battery supply chain would not only secure billions of euros in infrastructure funding but would also provide the quickest route to opening substantive accession negotiations.

It must be made absolutely clear that, to date, the newly elected team has not issued any official statement regarding the Jadar lithium mine project. Any assertion that the new government will ‘inevitably impose a total ban’ or ‘immediately and comprehensively reopen’ is premature from both a commercial and political perspective.

II. The Russian gas bill, renewed every three months: the hidden oil time bomb that Vučić defused ahead of schedule

If lithium mines are crucial to Serbia’s future, then natural gas and oil are what directly hold the entire country by the throat.

For a long time, the vast majority of Serbia’s natural gas supplies have come from Gazprom. In the geopolitical context following the Russia–Ukraine conflict, the Vučić government has leveraged its special relationship with Moscow to secure highly favourable energy terms for Serbia:

  • Short-term lock-up of shares: Throughout 2026, the gas supply agreement signed by Serbia and Russia remained in forceA rolling extension model involving ‘renewal every three months’(The contracts were renewed in March and June respectively until the end of September, and subsequently extended again until the end of 2026). Moscow categorically refused to sign a new long-term gas supply agreement, instead using frequent contract renewals to turn the gas tap into a political lever with which to exert pressure on Belgrade at any time;
  • Unbeatable price differences: According to figures disclosed by Vučić in September, the price of pipeline gas purchased by Serbia from Russia is approximately 318 euros per 1,000 cubic metres (€318/1,000 m³), which was slightly below the benchmark price on the European TTF spot market at the time. This relatively favourable price has kept thousands of factories in Serbia running and ensured that ordinary residents’ winter heating bills remain low.

Compared with the outstanding short-term natural gas contracts,In the oil refining sector, Vučić played an extremely astute move to hedge his risks on the eve of his election defeat：

As Serbia’s only modern oil refinery,The National Petroleum Industry of Serbia (NIS) supplies approximately 80 per cent of the country’s refined oil products. However, as Gazprom Neft and its affiliates have long held a majority stake in NIS (approximately 56 per cent), the company has, since In January 2025, the US Treasury Department’s Office of Foreign Assets Control (OFAC) added Gazprom Neft to the list of entities subject to sanctions relating to Russia.From the outset, it has been living under the shadow of sanctions; for over a year now, the entire island’s fuel supply has been teetering on a knife-edge, relying solely on rolling exemptions granted by the US.

20 October 2026 — Just five days before the general election, Serbia’s Deputy Prime Minister and Minister of Finance, Siniša Mali, suddenly made an official announcement：

The Russian shareholders have signed a binding share transfer agreement with the Hungarian multinational energy giant MOL Group, under which MOL will acquire the majority stake held by the Russian side; at the same time, the Serbian state’s shareholding has been increased to 35 per cent. The transaction is currently pending approval by the competition regulators in Serbia and Bosnia and Herzegovina.

This agreement, reached at the eleventh hour on the eve of the election, is of great practical significance:

Before leaving office, Vučić decisively defused this ‘secondary sanctions’ time bomb—which could have triggered a nationwide fuel shortage at any moment—for his successor. Bringing in a controlling stake from a company based in an EU Member State (Hungary’s MOL) whilst increasing the domestic public shareholding—should the transaction be successfully approved—would remove the main basis for US sanctions against NIS and ensure the continued operation of the Pančevo refinery during the transition of power.

The energy challenge facing the new government has thus been greatly simplified: the oil supply chain has largely been stabilised, whilst the real test will come this winter—when the short-term contract with Gazprom expires once again on 31 December— whether the new cabinet will embrace the EU’s common foreign policy in exchange for Brussels’ expensive and limited-capacity liquefied natural gas (LNG), or continue to hold its nose and head to the negotiating table in Moscow to secure a renewal at €318?

III. The Foundation of the ‘Rock-Solid Friendship’ between China and Serbia: A Concrete List Linking Industrialisation and Employment

Following the announcement of the election results, some Western media outlets raised the speculative question of whether ‘the ironclad friendship between China and Serbia might be undermined’. However, a close examination of Serbia’s macroeconomic fundamentals reveals an inescapable truth:Chinese investment is not an abstract political slogan, but a tangible presence that has long been deeply embedded in the very backbone of Serbia’s industrialisation.

Unlike the speculative capital circulating in financial markets, Chinese investment in Serbia is heavily concentrated in the manufacturing sector, the primary mining industry and strategic transport corridors, forming the bedrock of the country’s foreign trade exports and local tax revenue:

When analysing the aforementioned economic and trade cooperation, we must not only take an objective view of the narrative of Sino-Serbian friendship constructed by official media outlets such as Xinhua News Agency, but also draw rational conclusions based purely on commercial and employment considerations:

Based on reports from Chinese sources such as Xinhua News Agency and local media outlets such as serbia-business.eu,The number of jobs created locally by various medium- and large-scale investment projects from China runs into the “tens of thousands”(As reported).

From this, a relatively cautious business conclusion can be drawn:

The newly elected student movement may be closer to European values, but in the absence of any viable alternative, it would be difficult for any pragmatic government to single-handedly shut down a modern steelworks that supports the livelihoods of thousands of families, or to halt the expansion of a copper mine that is generating a stable cash flow. Chinese projects offer Serbia not abstract slogans, but tangible industrial assets and jobs.

In view of the current status of the project:The foundation of China–Serbia economic and trade relations is rooted more in the complementary nature of their real economies than in the personal will of any individual politician. In the foreseeable future, this cooperative framework is highly unlikely to change significantly, and is likely to become a key pillar supporting the new government’s efforts to stabilise domestic employment and public finances.

IV. The Macroeconomic Foundation and the 90-Day Test: The First Lesson in Governance for a Leaderless Team

In accordance with Serbian constitutional procedures, once the Electoral Commission has announced the official results and the new parliament has been convened, the winning coalition must, within The formation of the government will be formally completed within 90 days, otherwise the country will be forced to hold new general elections.

This youth and student movement, known as the ‘Faceless Group’, is about to face its first major test of real-world governance since its inception:

  1. The macroeconomic fundamentals still provide a safety net：
    In its latest assessment in mid-September 2026, the leading international credit rating agency S&P Global affirmed Serbia’s Long-term sovereign credit rating of BBB-(In October 2024, it was officially upgraded from speculative-grade BB+ to the lowest investment-grade category). Although real GDP growth for 2026 has been revised down slightly due to global geopolitical turmoil to 3.2%, and relaxed the deficit target to 3.5 per cent of GDP, but S&P explicitly pointed out that Serbia’s foreign exchange reserves are ample, its total government debt-to-GDP ratio is manageable (remaining below the 50 per cent safety threshold), and the social relief packages distributed prior to the election (accounting for approximately 1 per cent of GDP) have not undermined the country’s fiscal foundations;
  2. The Shift from the ‘Political Risk Discount’ to the ‘Policy Uncertainty Discount’：
    Why did sovereign bond yields hit a one-month low (6.42 per cent) following the election victory?
    This is because, in the secondary market, the years-long street standoffs, road blockades, school strikes and potential political instability have been factored in as an extremely high risk premium. The Students’ Alliance secured power with an absolute majority of more than half the seats, thereby averting a protracted ‘hung parliament’ crisis,Instantly wiped out the largest political risk premium in the short term；
  3. The real challenge will come once the policy has been introduced：
    However, as the applause dies down, the capital markets’ focus will shift seamlessly from ‘who takes office’ to ‘how they will govern’.
    As Fitch has warned:A young team with no clear cabinet line-up and no experienced Minister for Economic Affairs is highly likely to become bogged down in factional infighting and decision-making paralysis at an early stage.. Faced with the expiry of Rio Tinto’s lithium mining concession in 2027, the negotiations on natural gas supply guarantees that must be extended by the end of the year, and the massive infrastructure capital expenditure required for EXPO 2027, whether these young leaders can maintain an investment-grade credit rating amidst bureaucratic hurdles and external pressures will directly determine the sustainability of the current bond market rally.

Conclusion: Between idealism and the pull of reality

At the rally celebrating the election victory, young demonstrators sang the national anthem at the top of their voices, marking the end of an era. It was a moving moment in which the younger generation exercised their democratic voice through the ballot box.

But outside the building, the economic machine never stops turning:

Beneath the ground lies the Yadar lithium mine, in which Lituo has invested some 640 million euros and which has been included in the EU’s strategy on critical raw materials; through the pipelines flows Russian gas, which keeps industry running but whose supply is choked by short-term contracts renewed every three months; and on the banks of the Danube stand steelworks and mines revitalised by Chinese investment, which support the livelihoods of thousands of families.

Serbia’s national fortunes have never depended on whether it can make a decisive, either/or choice between East and West, but rather on whether it can, amidst the intricate web of major powers, carefully and shrewdly safeguard every last bit of its own development dividends.

For young people about to take up positions in government offices, their rite of passage into idealism begins with picking up these three heavy ledgers.
