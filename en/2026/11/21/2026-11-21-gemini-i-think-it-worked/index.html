A four-month delay, only to be met with the remark, ‘I think it worked’

A few months ago, many people still referred to Gemini as the ‘North American soya bean package’: cheap, high-volume and passable, but nobody considered it to be in the top tier. Recently, the tide has turned completely. New models have been released one after another, each more capable than the last, and they’ve managed to force their way into the ranks of cutting-edge models. During this time, I came across a conversation on X—spanning four months—which left quite an impression on me, so I wanted to write about it.

First, some background. The first half of this year was one of Google’s most difficult periods. At the developer conference at the start of the year, they stated that their flagship model, Gemini 3.5 Pro, would definitely be launched in June. However, it did not launch in June, nor was there any news in July. The reports emerging from outside the company were increasingly grim: internal code tests had failed to meet standards, the underlying architecture needed to be scrapped and rebuilt from scratch, and there were shareholder lawsuits and a sharp fall in the share price.

Just then, on 21 July (US time), Logan Kilpatrick, Product Manager at Google DeepMind, posted a tweet:

We have started our most ambitious pre-training run yet, for Gemini 4, and are excited by the progress : )

We’ve launched our most ambitious pre-training effort to date, in preparation for Gemini 4, and we’re really excited about the progress : )

That sounds all well and good. But with the 3.5 Pro still nowhere to be seen, a Gemini 4 has suddenly popped up—to anyone watching, it looks like nothing but empty promises. Tibo from the rival camp replied below:

Hope it finishes one day too!

I hope I’ll manage to finish this one one day too!

That ‘too’ is a bit of a dig. To put it plainly: you haven’t even finished drawing the last pie before you’ve drawn an even bigger one – good luck getting through it all in this lifetime.

If someone had rubbed salt into my wounds like that in public, I’d probably have been unable to resist firing back a few words. Logan simply replied:

Ty Tibo! TPU’s and Pre-training team are cooking!

Thanks, Tibo! The TPU and pre-training teams are working flat out!

I wasn’t angry, nor did I offer an explanation. Back then, the comments section was mostly filled with people there to have a laugh at Google’s expense, and some even left comments saying, ‘You lot can’t get anything right—it’s such a let-down.’

We’ve all seen what happened next. Google didn’t persist in saving face with the deadlocked 3.5 Pro, but instead channelled its resources into the next generation. Starting in late September, over the course of more than a month, Argon, Carbon and Hydrogen made successive appearances, followed by Gemini 4 Neon—which has been all over the news in the last couple of days and has outperformed its rivals in several benchmark tests—with XNeon and Sodium also lined up in the previews. Add to that the RSI, which is reportedly already in an advanced stage of development internally, and it’s fair to say Google has turned the tables on the competition.

At just after 3 am on 20 November, Logan dug out that tweet from four months ago—the one that had been mocked—and replied beneath his original post:

I think it worked

I think it’s worked.

There was no emotion behind it, nor was anyone tagged. Yet in the thread, this comment appeared directly above Tibo’s remark: ‘I hope this one gets finished one day too.’

Two minutes later, he added with a touch of self-deprecating humour: “Now, at last, we can launch the 3.5 Pro.”

After looking at these two images, I found myself quite moved. Whilst everyone loves a good underdog story featuring Silicon Valley bigwigs, I’m more interested in the two lessons contained within, which apply to us ordinary people too.

Firstly, when you’re being questioned, winning an argument is pointless; it’s only worth it if you get the job done.

Back in July, if Logan had spent ages arguing in the comments section that ‘our technology is actually really strong’, it would only have come across as feeble and laughable. He swallowed his pride; the team buried themselves in code and computing power for four months and got the job done. When the day finally came, there was no need for a loud rebuttal at all; a casual remark like ‘I reckon we’ve pulled it off’ spoke louder than any harsh words.

Secondly, in life and at work, there are often times when you need a bit of courage to ‘skip 3.5’.

We are all too easily held back by sunk costs. A project that is clearly unviable, or a relationship that has been draining us, is often pursued to the bitter end simply because we made a public commitment at the outset and fear the embarrassment of pulling out halfway through. The reason Google managed to turn things around this time is precisely because they acknowledged a temporary setback; rather than trying to patch up the leaky 3.5 Pro, they shifted their focus to launching Gemini 4, a much bigger venture.

If you’ve recently found yourself facing scepticism, or if you’ve put in a lot of effort but still haven’t seen any results—and are currently stuck in your own ‘lag phase’—why not reflect on this conversation to give yourself a bit of confidence? Don’t rush into arguing with anyone just yet, and don’t get too hung up on the version you’re currently stuck with. Keep your head down and get ready for your ‘next-generation model’. When the day comes that you’ve succeeded, you’ll probably be able to laugh off those very comments that once embarrassed you the most.
