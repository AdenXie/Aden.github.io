0. First, let me admit our mistake: we predicted the nodes, but we didn’t predict the script.

In Part IV, we outlined three possible outcomes for the closed-door briefings on items 11–18: a technical conclusion (55 per cent), escalation to a public hearing (30 per cent), and the initiation of legislative action (15 per cent).

The reality is that the briefing hadn’t even started yet when the main speaker had already left.

On 11–12, Jensen Huang resigned as CEO, President and Director of NVIDIA, with immediate effect. In other words, whilst we had correctly assessed the significance of that date, we had not in the slightest anticipated the possibility that ‘the hearing could not go ahead because the person being questioned was no longer in post’.

This incident in itself is worth including in the methodology:Scenario analysis can identify which days are significant, but it cannot identify the events that make up those days. The three branches we outlined—55/30/15—all shared the same implicit assumption: that Jensen Huang was still in office. This assumption was not included in any of the branches, as no one at the time believed it would change.This is the most typical way in which the scenario method fails: it is not a mistake in calculating probabilities, but rather an incomplete set of scenarios.

So this piece is not a defence, but a review.

I. The real reason for the resignation is not the rumours

The most crucial element on the timeline is not the dates of the scandals, but the dates of the parliamentary sessions.

Look at this table and note the interval between the two ‘increases’:

From 5 November, when key figures in Parliament were present, to 12 November, when they had left – exactly seven days.

The wording of the Board of Directors’ statement of 9 November is well worth reading word for word. It states that Jensen Huang is“Targets of foreign intelligence activities”, saying“There is no reason to believe that national security or NVIDIA’s confidential information has been compromised”, adding that Huang Renxun“I fully support this review”。

Please note the purpose of this statement: it is saying, ‘He is the victim, not the suspect’. This is the strongest protection a board can offer a CEO, and it also serves to pave the way for the 11–18 briefing—should anyone enquire, the company can say, ‘We’ve looked into it, and there’s nothing wrong with him’.

Three days later, the line was abandoned.

According to CNBC: the board had made the decision ‘the night before’

CNBC, citing sources, reported that the board of directors, following the announcement of the resignation,The night beforeThe decision had already been made; the team worked through the night to prepare a communication plan, finalised it the following morning, and announced that the CFO, Colette Kress, would take over on an interim basis.

The significance of this detail lies in the fact that:Decisions were made faster than the scandal could spread. 11 November: a second extramarital affair was exposed and the Senate requested documents; 12 November: by the morning, he had already stepped down—this does not look like a ‘well-considered and dignified exit’; it looks like cutting one’s losses.

Yet the official statement made no mention whatsoever of the scandal. The Chairman of the Board, Harvey Jones, said:“The Board has every confidence in Colette and the leadership team, and we expect the company’s operations, commitments to customers and product roadmap to continue without interruption.”(The Guardian has checked the full text of the announcement and confirmed that it contains no references to a scandal)

This is a standard template for a ‘civilised break-up’. It also makes one thing clear: the company does not intend to characterise this departure as an ethical issue, but rather as a risk management measure.

So what is the real risk?

If it were merely a rumour, the board could easily keep the CEO on and let him testify before Parliament himself—after all, his track record is impeccable and the product roadmap is set until 2028.

What forced the board to take action was the fact that the rumours and another development coincided in the same week. That line—see the next section.

The charges and the defence must be set out side by side

This is the part of this article where it is easiest to stray from the point. The same New York Times report also includes the other side’s account:Teter claims that it was Kessler who first granted Nvidia permission to make contact.

In other words, there are two narratives along this line:

  • Charges Version: NVIDIA’s legal team proactively telephoned the Ministry of Commerce to enquire about an investigation into one of its clients, following which the investigation was terminated or transferred—this constitutes a chain of suspicion of interference in law enforcement.
  • The Defence Version: The Ministry of Commerce granted the licence first; NVIDIA was simply following the procedure. Enquiring about the progress of the investigation is something any company with a compliance team would do.

We are unable to determine which version is correct, and this article does not make any such judgement. But one thing is clear:The focus of Congress’s inquiry is no longer on the CEO’s private life, but on ‘whether Nvidia interfered with investigations into its own clients’.

Why is this story more important than the rumours?

Because it links two things together:NVIDIA’s business practices (sales to China) with US National Security Procedures (Export Control Enforcement)。

An extramarital affair involving a CEO is, at worst, a personal embarrassment. But the fact that ‘the world’s most important chip company’s legal team rang the relevant authorities regarding an export control investigation into one of its own clients, after which the investigation was either terminated or referred elsewhere’—this is the sort of thing that could feature in a parliamentary hearing, be included in a subpoena, or find its way into draft legislation.

Jensen Huang’s true value has never been limited to his ability to manage NVIDIA.

III. His True Role: The Political Guarantor of Policy towards China

This section sets out the central argument of this article.

Jensen Huang is one of Trump’s closest advisers on AI policy and the most vocal advocate for chip sales to China. In May 2026, he travelled to Beijing with Trump on Air Force One—the significance of this detail lies not in the courtesy shown, but inHe himself is the very embodiment of the narrative of that journey.。

In that role, he fulfils three roles simultaneously:

  1. The company’s CEO: We must be accountable to our shareholders and ensure that revenue from the Chinese market continues to flow in.
  2. Policy adviser: To report to the White House and provide an industry assessment on whether to ‘sell or not to sell’.
  3. A buffer against political risk: When Congress asks, ‘Why are they still being sold?’, he is the one who can walk straight into the Oval Office to explain.

After 11–12, the third character disappeared.

There is no need to speculate on the consequences of this; a look at the mechanism is sufficient:

  • Export controls are being further tightened; no new legislation is required. Under the current rules (effective from 15 January 2026), H200-class chips are already subject to case-by-case approval.Approval criteria are a matter of administrative discretion; adopting criteria that do not take industry concerns into account would have the same effect as a ban, and would not require a vote in Parliament.
  • The combined efforts of Parliament and the Ministry of Justice have failed to achieve the desired balance. The person who could have acted as a buffer between ‘the President wanting to sell’ and ‘Parliament wanting to investigate’ is no longer there.
  • Lutnick is in a precarious position himself. The Senate Banking Committee has requested his documents, and both parties believe he should give evidence. The FT reported that he had linked the easing of restrictions on chip exports to China to a ‘deal with China’—This is no longer just a problem for Nvidia; it is a problem for the Ministry of Commerce.
  • The scope of the ‘circular trading’ investigation may be expanded. In its report, Reuters quoted a market participant directly as saying:“Nvidia is the circular-funder-in-chief; they’re the biggest by far… I assume that activity will moderate.” This is the first time the label ‘ringleader of circular financing’ has appeared in the mainstream media. It does not question the valuation, but ratherQuality of income。

So the statement in the headline needs to be made clear: the CEO’s position can be filled, but there is no substitute for a political patron. Kress will host the earnings call, but she will not speak to the President on behalf of Nvidia regarding export licences – that is not the role of a CFO, nor does it fall within the remit of an interim CEO.

Regarding ‘Tim Cook’s succession’

Trump mentioned Tim Cook several times on 11–12 and 11–13, saying, “A Legend replaces a Legend!”, and describing Cook as a “great candidate”.

It must be made clear that this is the President’s personal opinion, not a Board procedure. There are no reports to suggest that the board has approached Cook. It is incorrect to write that ‘a successor has emerged’.

About Kress

Colette Kress has served as NVIDIA’s CFO for many years and is a key figure in the company’s financial and capital markets communications.

However, there are no public reports to suggest that she has ever held the position of CEO or head of a business division. We have not been able to independently verify the exact date on which she took up the post of CFO; this has therefore been listed as a gap.

Whether she is seen as a ‘stabilising force’ or a ‘caretaker’ will depend entirely on the length of the search for a successor. If a successor is appointed within a month, she will be seen as a transitional figure; if the process drags on until next year, the markets will begin to ask, ‘Who is making decisions on products and production capacity?’—and this is precisely one of the questions the 11–17 financial report is intended to answer.

Three grounds for the upward revision of the break-even rate

  1. The CEO position at the world’s leading chip manufacturer is vacant, and there is no clear successor. The board says it is considering both internal and external candidates, but there have been no reliable reports naming any specific candidates. The duration of this vacuum is uncertain, yet capital expenditure decisions cannot be put on hold.
  2. Export controls are being further tightened; no new legislation is required. Under the current rules, approvals are already granted on a case-by-case basis, with the criteria for approval subject to administrative discretion. When political pressure mounts, all it takes is an internal directive.
  3. Private equity funding is slowing down. OpenAI has raised $11 billion, which is only about a third of its original target (up to $30 billion). If funding in the private market is drying up, the fulfilment rate of computing power commitments will have to be scaled back.

Why not be more pessimistic?

  1. Anthropic was oversubscribed by approximately six times—The strongest evidence on the demand side: an order book backed by real money.
  2. CoreWeave raises its full-year guidance to $13.3 billion in Q3—The order backlog has not collapsed.
  3. Kress is the long-term CFO; short-term market communication risks are manageable—She knows every figure relating to this line of business.
  4. The ‘liquidation event’ theory makes sense: Jeremiah Buckley of Janus Henderson described this resignation as“clearing event”(clearance event), adding that the company’s product roadmap “has been finalised through to 2028”. It is better to get all the bad news out of the way in one go than to drag it out.

Don’t overlook the other side either

Ann Lipton, a professor of corporate law at the University of Colorado, takes the opposite view:The resignation “underscores the seriousness of the allegations” and will strengthen the shareholders’ arguments regarding damages, the materiality of the misrepresentations and the likelihood that Mr Huang was aware of them. ‘Hundreds of investors’ have already contacted the law firm.

The disagreement between these two schools of thought lies not in the facts, but in the timescale.: Buckley looks to see when share prices will bottom out, whilst Lipton looks to see when the litigation will begin.

Six-point checklist (items 5 and 6 have been added this time):

  1. Has the data centre met its revenue target?—With $108 billion as the benchmark, this is the item that is easiest to quantify and on which there is the least disagreement.
  2. Will the gross profit margin hold steady at 74 per cent?— More important than revenue. Blackwell/Rubin’s ramp-up costs and HBM costs will be reflected in this line; a decline in gross margin indicates a loss of pricing power.
  3. Will China’s figures be included again?—From 15 January 2026, the BIS will subject H200-class chips to a case-by-case approval process. The guidance issued last quarter explicitly excluded revenue from computing power in Chinese data centres.
  4. Customer Concentration and Allegations of ‘Circular Trading’—Now that the media has coined the term ‘circular-funder-in-chief’, this issue is likely to come under even greater scrutiny than before.
  5. (New) The credibility of guidance under the leadership of an interim CEO—As Kress is a former CFO, the market will be asking, ‘Who is making the decisions on products and production capacity?’
  6. (New) Are export licensing and compliance risks included in the ‘Risk Factors’ section of the 10-Q?—The termination of the Ministry of Commerce’s investigation, the DOJ taking over, and the congressional subpoena: are these three matters included in the statutory disclosures?

Items 5 and 6 have only appeared this week, and this is where this financial report differs most from previous ones: whereas previously we looked at the figures, this time we also need to consider ‘who is accountable for those figures’.
