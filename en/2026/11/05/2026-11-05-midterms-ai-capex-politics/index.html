On 3–4 November 2026, as the dust settled on the US mid-term elections—with the Democrats sweeping to victory and regaining control of both the Senate and the House of Representatives—political variables officially came to the fore. Major media outlets rushed to run headlines proclaiming, ‘With Congress changing hands, a regulatory winter for AI is upon us’. However, if one truly delves into the structure of power distribution in the United States, one discovers a starkly contrasting reality:The US Congress in Washington, which is under intense media scrutiny, is in fact ‘toothless’; it is the state governors, public utility commissions and local votes that truly hold the power to strangle data centres’ return to positive cash flow.

As the counting of votes across the states was completed overnight, the Democrats staged a complete turnaround, securing control of both the Senate and the House of Representatives. Before the market opened, US equities presented a starkly polarised picture, with some sectors soaring whilst others plummeted:

Capital has delivered its final verdict through its actions:After a swift assessment, Wall Street concluded that a divided Congress would merely result in a political squabble consisting of hearings and subpoenas, and by no means a death sentence capable of substantially curtailing capital expenditure on AI.

I. A Misalignment in the Power Structure: The Federation is toothless, whilst the states wield the knife

Many tech investors have fallen into a mindset: seeing the Democrats regain control of both the Senate and the House of Representatives, they assume that sweeping legislation to curb the power of tech giants or introduce taxes on data centres will soon be introduced in Washington.

This is a serious misinterpretation of the system. To gain a clear understanding of future policy resistance, one must examine the instruments of power at the federal and state levels separately:

Figure 2A map of regulatory powers over AI data centres in the US: Federal legislation is constrained by a 60-vote threshold, which primarily manifests as investigative noise; the real power to halt projects and control costs lies entirely with governors’ authorisations, PUC electricity tariff allocations and local referendums.

1. Federal Parliament: Subpoenas and microphones are loud, but there is no direct right to call a strike

The Democrats’ landslide victory in Congress has indeed shifted the balance of power, but their legislative clout faces an insurmountable constitutional ceiling:

  • The Senate’s 60-vote rule is unbreakable: Although the Democrats have secured a majority in the Senate (with a seat ratio of at least 54:42, having flipped seven seats), they remain a long way from the 60-vote threshold required to pass major, controversial bills—the so-called ‘filibuster-proof’ majority. The Republicans’ hold on at least 42 seats is sufficient to block any radical restrictive legislation targeting the technology sector;
  • The real ‘teeth’ of the new Parliament lie in subpoenas and public inquiries: The chairs of the House committees are set to change hands. According to Reuters, citing an internal briefing from a senior Democratic aide in the Senate, the top priority for the new Congress will be“Private equity investment, tax loopholes relating to data centres, and the impact of high computing power on the cost of living for residents”. Congress could issue subpoenas to summon the CEOs of tech giants to take the witness stand, using public hearings to generate a flurry of media headlines and undermine the valuation premium on tech shares in the secondary market;
  • Progress on existing legislation has been minimal: Although members of Congress have previously introduced targeted bills (such as H.R. 6529, which calls on the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission to convene a hearing on residential electricity price protection, and H.R. 8241, which explored the establishment of a dedicated grid connection queue for data centres), in this divided Congress, these are all symbolic bills characterised by a large number of proposals but very little momentum for substantive implementation;
  • A vacuum in federal executive power: The White House’s executive order on AI, issued in July 2025, essentially only provides accelerated federal licensing approval for large-scale facilities exceeding 100 MW or with an investment of over US$500 million,It has absolutely no right to interfere with the states’ autonomy in land use, commercial zoning and residential electricity pricing.; whereas the ‘Taxpayer Electricity Price Protection Pledge’ issued by the White House in March 2026 relied entirely on voluntary participation by businesses and contained no statutory penalty provisions.

2. State and local levels: they hold the real power to cut off cash flow

It is the state capitals and local counties and districts—thousands of miles away from Wall Street—that are the true ‘realms of the underworld’ determining whether data centres can begin construction and be connected to the grid. No matter how high their valuation—even in the hundreds of billions—data centres are, in the physical world, nothing more than colossal, energy-guzzling structures. Their lifeline is entirely dependent on three local channels:

  1. The Governor’s Executive Orders and Environmental Licensing Powers: The Governor may directly instruct the State Environmental Protection Agency to suspend air quality permits (which primarily limit the total emissions from standby diesel generators) and industrial water abstraction licences;
  2. Cost Review of Electricity Tariffs by the State Public Utilities Commission (PUC/PSC): This is the most devastating blow. The members of the Regulatory Commission hold the power of final adjudication and may, in accordance with the law, issue a binding ruling:The infrastructure upgrade costs—running into billions of dollars—for the new high-voltage substations and ultra-high-voltage transmission grids built to accommodate the explosive growth of data centres must be borne entirely by the technology developers themselves; it is strictly forbidden to pass these costs on to residential households or ordinary commercial electricity meters!
  3. Veto power over referendums in local constituencies: County-level city councils and local voters can directly amend land-use classifications through a referendum, thereby adding data centres to the list of prohibited developments.

One sentence that gets to the heart of the matter:The new Congress in Washington has unleashed a ‘valuation storm’ of hearings and subpoenas; whilst state governors, public utility commissioners and local voters have cut off the ‘real cash flow’ generated by data centres coming online.

II. A genuine political earthquake: three major computing power strongholds turn blue and face cross-party resistance

In these mid-term elections, the real ‘grey swan’ is not to be found on Capitol Hill, but in the sweeping shake-up of state governorships and utility management.

The three key hubs for computing power—which boast the highest density of data centre construction and the most concentrated land use in the United States—all experienced historic upsets on the same day:

  • Texas: Democrat Gina Hinojosa defeated the long-serving Greg Abbott,This has brought a definitive end to the Republicans’ 30-year stranglehold on the Texas governorship! Meanwhile, James Talarico was successfully elected, becoming the first Democratic state senator in Texas since 1994;
  • Georgia: Keisha Lance Bottoms, the former mayor of Atlanta, has been successfully elected as governor, bringing an end to the Republicans’ more than 20-year hold on power in Georgia. Meanwhile, industry data from CBRE confirms that,The Atlanta metropolitan area in Georgia is currently the most dynamic and active core market in the United States in terms of data centre capacity under construction.；
  • Ohio: Amy Acton, a Democrat who previously served as State Health Director, defeated the prominent conservative figure Vivek Ramaswamy to be elected Governor. The area around Columbus, Ohio, is precisely the core cluster where Amazon AWS and Intel have made significant investments.

The counterintuitive truth: opposition to data centres has never been an issue exclusive to the Democratic Party

Many market analysts instinctively assume that a Democratic administration will oppose data centres, whilst a Republican administration will give them the green light.

However, the actual legislative records reveal a fact that has shocked Silicon Valley:Fears and backlash regarding the expansion of data centres have long since evolved into a highly bipartisan political consensus across the United States!

  • The precedent set by the early primary in Texas, a Republican stronghold：
    More than a month before this general election, 21 September 2026… Abbott, then the Republican governor, signed a landmark executive order directly on the state government’s official website—A complete halt has been placed on the issuing of environmental permits for all new data centres across the province, ordering that the Texas Reliability Council (ERCOT) must first complete a thorough audit and assessment of the state’s power grid and water resources; On 14 September, Texas even imposed a deadline requiring data centres to submit detailed reports on their substantial water usage, and on 24 September, the State Attorney General launched an investigation into data centre development to ensure transparency in water usage reporting and compliance with water resource regulations!
  • Lanzhou has long had stringent legislation in place：
    New York State’s Democratic Governor Hochul signed Executive Order No. 62 as early as July 2026, establishing the first state-level moratorium in the United States:For a full year, no land or environmental permits shall be issued for new data centres with a capacity of 50 megawatts or more (≥50MW); Illinois Governor Pritzker also directly suspended the substantial special tax credit scheme previously in place for data centres.

A rock-solid wall of voter opposition: an overwhelming 80 per cent to 90 per cent voted in favour of local building bans

On 3 November, election day, as many as 41 local initiatives directly related to data centres These issues have been put to the voters in referendums (according to statistics from the renowned political data organisation Ballotpedia, a total of 50 such measures have been tracked across various regions this year). Of these, 17 measures directly call for a complete or size-based ban on the construction of new data centres, whilst three require an immediate and unconditional moratorium on construction lasting at least six months.

Although the final vote counts following the general election are still being confirmed in due course by the county electoral commissions, the early referendum results finalised prior to the general election have already revealed a reality of public opinion that is sending shivers down the spines of property developers:

  • A proposal to ban the construction of data centres in Monterey Park, California, with the aim of 88.3% was formally enshrined in law following an overwhelming majority in favour (with over 10,000 residents voting in favour of the construction ban);
  • The building ban in Canterbury, New Hampshire, was introduced by 83.6% was passed by an overwhelming majority;
  • Support for the suspension order in Nottingham, New Hampshire, has soared to a staggering 90.3%！

Regardless of which party local voters support in the presidential election, when faced with ‘a colossal structure being built on their doorstep that buzzes, drains the groundwater and puts the nearby electricity grid under strain’,Local communities across the United States are building a physical barrier—with a remarkably consistent 80 per cent to 90 per cent vote against—that cannot be easily brought down by Wall Street capital.

III. Who Is Most Vulnerable: The Divergence Among Major Firms and the Cruel Prolongation of Cash Flow Lags

At our meeting on 31 October,‘In the same week, macro and micro perspectives delivered conflicting verdicts on AI capital expenditure’In a previous article, we pointed out that the sell-off in the secondary market following the earnings reports of the ‘Big Tech’ companies essentially reflected a loss of patience with ‘spending hundreds of billions whilst suffering severe outflows of free cash flow’ (for example, Amazon’s net free cash outflow of US$42.4 billion over the past 12 months).

Now that political factors have come into play,Whilst it has not directly eliminated the genuine business need for AI, it has created an extremely damaging external shock—‘regulatory lag’.。

Every hearing initiated in the state legislature, every heated debate before the Public Utilities Commission regarding electricity cost-sharing schemes, and every environmental impact assessment lawsuit faced at local county and municipal levels directly affects the timing of a data centre’s commissioning and the generation of cloud revenue.Delaying it by 6 to 18 months. For companies that rely on borrowing or drawing on their cash flow to purchase computing power, a prolongation of this cycle is tantamount to a further deterioration in free cash flow.

In the face of this new storm that has flared up at the local level, there is a sharp divergence in risk resilience within the technology sector:

Data facts that challenge the narrative: the disconnect between statistics and politics

When discussing the politics of electricity pricing, it is essential to take seriously an empirical finding disclosed in a report by the Congressional Research Service (CRS R48646) and carried out by the authoritative Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory (LBNL):

Multidimensional analyses of the US electricity market between 2019 and 2025 indicate that the primary driver of residential electricity price rises across the country is investment in the replacement of the ageing transmission and distribution networks; In many states where data centre loads have experienced explosive growth, the average cost per kilowatt-hour for ordinary households has, statistically speaking, actually shown a slight decline, as major industrial electricity consumers, who are charged at fixed rates, have absorbed a significant portion of the depreciation costs for the underlying grid infrastructure.

However,In the cut-throat world of political elections, statistical patterns are never a match for voters’ gut feelings.。

When candidates for the Arizona Public Service Commission highlighted the costs that the local electricity company (APS) had passed on to residents between 2023 and 2025, US$587 million ($587M) When the breakdown of costs is made public, politicians have found the perfect weapon with which to deflect public anger. ‘Data centres are driving up the public’s electricity bills’ has become an unshakeable political reality.

VI. Honest Explanation of Data Definitions and Factual Boundaries

When reading this report, readers are advised to maintain a clear understanding of its scope:

  1. Statement by the newly elected governor on ‘suspending data centres’: The relevant proposals put forward by the newly elected governors of Texas, Georgia and Ohio, are currently reflected primarily in the political positions they have put forward at campaign rallies and in their policy manifestos. Whether these will be translated into formal, legally binding gubernatorial executive orders or threshold restrictions (such as the establishment of a 50 MW threshold) remains to be confirmed until after they are officially sworn in on 1 January 2027;
  2. Final results of the vote count for the 41 local referendums: As local county election offices in the United States are subject to a statutory public notice period for the final official verification of ballot measures, the figures cited in the main text of this report—Monterey Park (88.3 per cent) and Canterbury (83.6 per cent)—are representative samples for which the legal verification process was completed around the time of the election, and do not constitute any subjective speculation regarding the undisclosed national average pass rate;
  3. Party affiliation regarding the proposed federal bill: H.R. 6529 and H.R. 8241, as mentioned in the main text, are draft bills with public congressional numbers. Specific changes to their cross-party sponsorship and the latest status of their committee deliberations are subject to the pace of updates within the congressional system; they are provided here solely as background information on legislative developments;
  4. Explanation of the dual measurement standards for the amount of bonds issued by Meta: Regarding Meta’s bond issuance during the election cycle, media outlets such as CNBC reported that its initial target range was between US$20 billion and US$25 billion, whilst authoritative sources such as Singapore’s *Business Times* tracked that it ultimately secured US$15 billion across five bond tranches; this information has been presented side-by-side in the analysis;

Conclusion: The edifice of computing power must ultimately be rooted in the earth

Amidst the fervent narratives of the tech industry, the giants of Silicon Valley often harbour the illusion that they transcend geographical and institutional boundaries: that as long as they possess computing power, algorithms and capital cheques worth tens of billions of dollars, they can reshape the entire physical world through the sheer will of code alone.

However, the dramatic mid-term elections of 2026 provided all tech enthusiasts with a most vivid lesson in American constitutional politics.

On Wall Street, investment banks can assign a valuation premium of several billion dollars to a 100-megawatt data centre; at the White House, executive orders can make grand pledges regarding technological leadership.But when the roar of giant cooling towers shatters the tranquillity of the countryside, and when tens of thousands of servers voraciously consume groundwater, and when neighbours’ electricity bills quietly soar due to the ultra-high-voltage grid upgrade, those ordinary voters who previously had nothing to do with artificial intelligence will not hesitate to cast their votes, erecting one cold, impenetrable wall after another before the county council, the public utilities commission and the governor’s residence.

No matter how vast the cloud computing power may be, the foundations of data centres must ultimately be laid on solid ground.

For the AI giants charging ahead at breakneck speed, learning to humble themselves in the face of local voters—who hold the reins of water meters, electricity meters and voting machines—may well be a true test that is more difficult, and indeed more protracted, than retraining a trillion-parameter large language model.
